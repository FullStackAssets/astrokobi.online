The Sun woke from a quiet spell on November 17, unleashing two M‑class flares that briefly disrupted radio communications. The first, an M1.1 flare at 15:49 UTC, produced a minor R1 blackout over Brazil, and the second, an M1.7 at 21:39 UTC, caused another R1 blackout near Easter Island in the Pacific. Both eruptions came from active region AR4578, which also generated six of the period’s twenty flares. A coronal mass ejection (CME) tied to the M1.1 flare is expected to brush Earth on November 22, while a separate CME from a C7.3 flare in AR4579 will miss us entirely. In a separate milestone, Voyager 1 crossed the one‑light‑day distance from Earth, about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion km), making a round‑trip light signal take two days.

## What happened
Active region AR4578 produced an M1.1 flare at 15:49 UTC, triggering an R1 radio blackout over Brazil, and an M1.7 flare at 21:39 UTC, causing a second R1 blackout over the Pacific near Easter Island. The M1.1 flare also launched a CME whose trajectory points partly toward Earth, with a predicted glancing impact on November 22.

A nearby region, AR4579, emitted a C7.3 flare that expelled its own CME, but space‑weather models show that this ejection will not intersect Earth’s orbit. Overall, the Sun recorded twenty flares during the reporting period: two M‑class and eighteen C‑class, marking a sharp rise from the preceding lull of only B‑ and C‑class events.

Meanwhile, Voyager 1 achieved a historic distance of one light‑day from Earth, roughly 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion km). At light speed, a signal now takes a full day to reach the probe and another day for a reply, underscoring the vastness of interstellar space.

## Why it matters
The CME headed for Earth, even as a glancing blow, can induce minor geomagnetic disturbances that may enhance auroral displays at high latitudes and modestly affect satellite drag. The R1 radio blackouts, though brief, illustrate how moderate solar events can temporarily degrade HF communications used by aviation and maritime operators. Voyager 1’s distance milestone provides a real‑time benchmark for deep‑space communication latency, informing future interstellar probe designs and mission planning.

## How to think about it
Treat the November 22 CME as a low‑to‑moderate risk event: monitor NOAA and space‑weather alerts, schedule non‑critical HF communications outside the predicted impact window, and be prepared to adjust satellite orbit predictions if drag increases. For Voyager 1, treat the two‑day round‑trip light‑time as a baseline when designing command sequences for probes that will travel beyond the heliosphere.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the CME will cause a major geomagnetic storm; current models predict only a peripheral encounter with limited effects.</Callout>
