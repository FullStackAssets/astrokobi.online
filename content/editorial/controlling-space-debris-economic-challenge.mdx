Space debris is no longer a distant nuisance; it is a daily operational challenge for megaconstellations. A new pre‑print by NASA JPL scientist Slava Turyshev reveals that Starlink satellites performed 144,404 collision‑avoidance maneuvers between December 2024 and May 2025, roughly one dodge per satellite each week. The study argues that the crisis is rooted in control and economic incentives, not merely in the ability to scoop up trash. As dead rocket stages linger at 850 km, they dominate long‑term collision risk, threatening both commercial services and scientific missions. Understanding this shift reshapes policy, business models, and the engineering of future space traffic.

<Callout type="takeaway">Controlling orbital debris requires economic incentives and active traffic management, not just removal.</Callout>

## What happened
Dr. Turyshev’s paper, posted on arXiv, compiles FCC filings that show SpaceX’s Starlink constellation executed 144,404 collision‑avoidance burns from December 2024 through May 2025, meaning each satellite in the 500‑600 km band has to maneuver roughly once a week to stay clear of other objects. The analysis separates the densely populated low‑Earth orbit (LEO) used by megaconstellations from the higher “legacy” zone around 850 km, where large defunct rocket bodies and spent stages have little atmospheric drag and can remain for centuries.

European Space Agency data cited in the study indicate that about 96 percent of the long‑term collision risk originates from these dead objects in the higher zone. Even if all new launches stopped tomorrow, the existing debris cloud would continue to fragment faster than natural decay can clear it, creating a cascading Kessler‑like scenario.

Beyond the physics, the paper emphasizes that without clear economic mechanisms—such as liability fees, active de‑orbit mandates, or market‑based incentives—operators lack motivation to invest in debris‑avoidance technologies or end‑of‑life disposal, turning the orbital environment into a commons tragedy.

## Why it matters
The stakes span commercial broadband providers, national security satellites, and scientific observatories that rely on clean sky windows. Unchecked debris growth threatens to raise insurance premiums, force redesigns of satellite constellations, and potentially limit access to valuable orbital regimes. Policymakers must therefore craft regulations that internalize the external costs of leaving junk in space, while engineers need tools for real‑time traffic coordination and affordable de‑orbit solutions.

## How to think about it
Treat orbital space as a shared resource with a market‑based commons model. First, assess the economic externalities of each satellite launch—estimate the expected collision cost over its lifetime. Second, apply a tiered liability structure that scales with orbital altitude and object size, making high‑risk debris more expensive to operate. Third, integrate automated conjunction assessment into mission design so avoidance maneuvers become a routine, low‑cost activity. Finally, invest in scalable de‑orbit technologies (e.g., drag sails, electrodynamic tethers) that become standard payload items, turning compliance into a cost‑neutral design choice.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely solely on post‑launch debris removal without first establishing launch‑license conditions and active de‑orbit incentives.</Callout>
