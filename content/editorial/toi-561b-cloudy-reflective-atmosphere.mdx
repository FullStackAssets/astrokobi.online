The James Webb Space Telescope has confirmed that the ultra‑short‑period super‑Earth TOI‑561 b, long thought to be a barren lava world, actually sports a dense, cloudy atmosphere. Located 280.5 light‑years away in Sextans, the planet circles its star every 10.5 hours, heating its dayside to over 2,000 K. Webb’s infrared spectra reveal high‑altitude haze that reflects a large fraction of stellar heat back into space. This finding overturns the prevailing view that such scorching, close‑in rocky planets cannot retain substantial gases. Understanding how TOI‑561 b holds onto its veil could reshape models of atmospheric loss.

<Callout type="takeaway">TOI‑561 b proves even the hottest, closest super‑Earths can cloak themselves in reflective clouds.</Callout>

## What happened
In October 2026, a team led by the University of Montreal re‑observed TOI‑561 b with JWST, targeting the planet’s secondary eclipse to isolate its thermal emission. The resulting spectra displayed a broad, featureless slope consistent with scattering particles high in the atmosphere, interpreted as clouds or haze.

The researchers published the results in The Astronomical Journal (Sept 29 2026), noting that the observed albedo is much higher than expected for a bare lava surface. The haze appears to act as a “sunscreen,” reflecting stellar radiation and potentially moderating surface temperatures on the planet’s night side.

## Why it matters
If a super‑Earth so close to its star can retain a thick, reflective envelope, current photo‑evaporation models for ultra‑short‑period planets may be incomplete. The clouds could alter the planet’s energy budget, affecting surface cooling and the chemistry of any residual volatiles. This discovery forces theorists to incorporate high‑temperature condensate clouds into atmospheric loss simulations, with implications for the habitability assessments of other close‑in rocky worlds.

## How to think about it
When evaluating exoplanet atmospheres, factor in that high‑temperature condensates can form reflective layers; update thermal evolution models to include cloud albedo effects; and treat ultra‑short‑period planets as potential outliers rather than rule‑breakers, using a case‑by‑case approach.

<Callout type="warning">The current data are limited to broadband spectra; conclusions about cloud composition and global climate remain provisional.</Callout>
