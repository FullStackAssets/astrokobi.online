The Leonid meteor shower returns this year, with its peak predicted for 0 UTC on November 18, 2026. Observers in the Northern Hemisphere will have the best chance between midnight and dawn on the mornings of November 17 and 18, when the radiant climbs high in the eastern sky. The shower is notable not only for its steady display of 10–15 meteors per hour under dark conditions, but also for its historic storms that have produced thousands of meteors per minute. With Jupiter and Mars flanking the radiant, the display promises a striking backdrop for skywatchers.

<Callout type="takeaway">The 2026 Leonids offer a modest peak but a chance to glimpse a meteor stream linked to dramatic historic storms.</Callout>

## What happened
The peak is set for 0 UTC on November 18, 2026, and the optimal viewing window spans the midnight‑to‑dawn hours of November 17 and 18. The radiant rises around midnight and reaches its highest point just before dawn, so meteors will appear across the sky even if you do not stare directly at the radiant. A 1st‑quarter Moon at 11:48 UTC on November 17 sets around midnight, leaving the sky largely free of moonlight.

Earth will be traveling through the Leonid stream from October 28 through December 7, meaning occasional Leonids can be seen any night in that span. Under ideal dark‑sky conditions with no moon, observers can expect 10 to 15 meteors per hour at the peak.

The Leonids are famed for periodic storms; historical records cite rates up to 2,500 meteors per minute (about 150,000 per hour) and brief bursts of 20 to 70 meteors per second during the 1833 and 1966 storms. These storms tend to recur in cycles of 33 to 34 years, adding a layer of anticipation for this year's display.

## Why it matters
Even a modest meteor shower engages the public with tangible space phenomena, fostering interest in astronomy and planetary science. The proximity of Jupiter and Mars to the radiant offers a rare visual cue that can help novices locate the shower and learn about planetary positions. For scientists, each annual pass through the stream provides data on dust particle distribution and the dynamics of cometary debris.

However, expectations shaped by historic storms can lead to disappointment if observers anticipate dramatic fireballs. Understanding the difference between a regular peak and a storm helps set realistic goals and encourages responsible sky‑watching practices.

## How to think about it
1. Check the local weather forecast for clear, dark skies on the nights of November 17‑18.
2. Choose a viewing site far from city lights; even modest light pollution will drown a 10‑15‑meteor‑per‑hour shower.
3. Allow your eyes 20‑30 minutes to adapt to darkness and use a wide‑field view rather than a telescope.
4. Scan the sky, not just the radiant; meteors will streak from the radiant but appear anywhere.
5. Take note of Jupiter and Mars as reference points to confirm you are looking in the right region.

<Callout type="warning">Avoid planning observations from heavily light‑polluted areas; the faint Leonid meteors will be invisible against city glow.</Callout>
