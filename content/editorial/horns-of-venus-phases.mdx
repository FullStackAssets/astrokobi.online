Tonight’s Astronomy Picture of the Day reveals Venus framed as a luminous crescent with two bright extensions that look like horns. The image captures the planet positioned almost directly between the Sun and Earth, a geometry that repeats only about every 1.5 years. This back‑lit view highlights Venus’s thick atmosphere scattering sunlight, creating an arc that stretches beyond a half‑circle. Observers with modest binoculars can spot the horns during twilight, while the keen‑eyed may even glimpse them unaided. The phenomenon links modern skywatchers to ancient cultures that recorded Venus’s changing brightness.

<Callout type=\"takeaway\">When Venus sits between Earth and the Sun, its atmosphere creates a glowing “horned” crescent visible with modest optics.</Callout>

## What happened
The planet appears as a thin, illuminated crescent flanked by two bright extensions that extend past 180 degrees; astronomers refer to these extensions as the “horns.” This configuration occurs when Venus lies almost directly between the Sun and Earth, a line‑up that happens roughly every one and a half years, mirroring the monthly new‑Moon alignment but with a bright, refracted arc instead of darkness.

Ancient Babylonian and Maya records note Venus’s brightness changes, hinting that early observers may have witnessed similar horned phases. Modern observers can see the effect with a small telescope or binoculars during twilight, and under exceptionally clear skies and sharp eyesight, the horns may be discernible without aid.

## Why it matters
The horned view provides a vivid illustration of atmospheric scattering, a key process that shapes planetary climates and informs exoplanet studies. It also offers a tangible link between contemporary amateur astronomy and millennia‑old skywatching traditions, reinforcing how celestial events bridge science and culture. Finally, the predictable timing gives educators a concrete example to teach orbital geometry and phase cycles.

## How to think about it
Plan your observation for the hour after sunset or before sunrise when Venus is low on the horizon and the sky is still dim. Use binoculars or a small telescope to enhance the contrast of the crescent and its horns, but never point any optics directly at the Sun. Record the angle of the horns and compare them over successive alignments to appreciate the subtle changes in Venus’s phase. Treat the event as a practical lesson in orbital mechanics: the same geometry that creates a new Moon also produces this bright back‑lit arc.

<Callout type=\"warning\">Never look directly at the Sun through binoculars or a telescope; always keep the Sun out of the field of view to protect your eyes.</Callout>
