A team of astronomers has identified J1602+4228AB, the earliest known compact pair of quasars, existing just 900 million years after the Big Bang. The duo, nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, was uncovered using Gemini North and the James Webb Space Telescope. Its extreme proximity—only a few kiloparsecs apart—makes it the most compact quasar pair seen from the cosmic dawn era. Because quasars are powered by supermassive black holes, finding two massive, actively feeding black holes so early challenges our understanding of how such objects can grow in less than a billion years. The discovery therefore forces a rethink of early‑universe black‑hole formation models.
<Callout type="takeaway">The existence of a massive, tightly bound quasar pair at 900 Myr forces a revision of black‑hole growth theories.</Callout>
## What happened
The international team reported the system J1602+4228AB in the Aether survey, locating it at a redshift corresponding to roughly 900 million years after the Big Bang. Gemini North’s near‑infrared spectrograph first flagged the object as a double source, and follow‑up imaging with JWST confirmed two luminous nuclei separated by about 2 kiloparsecs.
Both nuclei exhibit the characteristic broad emission lines and extreme luminosities of quasars, indicating black‑hole masses of several hundred million solar masses each. Their separation is an order of magnitude smaller than previously known quasar pairs from the same epoch, making the pair the most compact example from cosmic dawn.
The findings appear in an Astrophysical Journal Letters paper titled “Nāpōwawā’enakaulua: a close quasar pair at cosmic dawn in the Aether survey,” with Silvia Onorato listed as the lead author.
## Why it matters
The pair provides a rare, direct window onto supermassive black‑hole assembly during the universe’s first billion years, a period when theoretical models struggle to produce black holes above 10⁸ M☉ so quickly. Its compactness suggests that major galaxy mergers and the resulting gas inflows may have been far more efficient at feeding black holes than current simulations assume. If such systems are common, they could imply that the seeds of today’s massive black holes formed earlier or grew by mechanisms beyond simple accretion, reshaping our picture of early galaxy evolution.

## How to think about it
Treat this discovery as a benchmark case: when evaluating early‑universe black‑hole scenarios, compare predicted mass‑growth tracks against the observed ~10⁸ M☉ masses and sub‑kiloparsec separations. Incorporate merger‑driven gas inflow efficiencies into semi‑analytic models, and use JWST follow‑up spectroscopy to refine mass and accretion‑rate estimates for similar objects. By building a larger sample of compact quasar pairs, the community can assess whether Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua is an outlier or a sign of a broader, previously hidden population.
<Callout type="warning">Do not treat this lone discovery as proof that all early‑universe black‑hole models are wrong; further surveys are required to assess how common such systems are.</Callout>
