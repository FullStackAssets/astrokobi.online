A team of international astronomers has identified J1602+4228AB, nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, the earliest and most compact quasar pair ever seen, existing just 900 million years after the Big Bang. The pair was uncovered using deep imaging from the Gemini North telescope and spectroscopic data from the James Webb Space Telescope. Their separation is only a few kiloparsecs, making them the tightest duo observed from the cosmic dawn era. Because quasars are powered by supermassive black holes, their existence at such an early epoch forces a rethink of how quickly these black holes can grow.

<Callout type="takeaway">The discovery shows that massive black holes were already in place less than a billion years after the universe began, challenging current growth models.</Callout>

## What happened
The researchers reported the object in a paper titled “Nāpōwawā'enakaulua: a close quasar pair at cosmic dawn in the Aether survey” in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*. Gemini North imaging revealed two point sources separated by roughly 3 kiloparsecs, and follow‑up spectroscopy with JWST confirmed both sources exhibit broad emission lines characteristic of active quasars. Mass estimates based on their luminosities suggest each black hole exceeds a few hundred million solar masses.

The pair’s redshift places it at a cosmic age of about 900 million years, well within the epoch known as cosmic dawn when the first galaxies and supermassive black holes were emerging. The discovery is notable because theoretical models predict that building such massive black holes so early requires sustained, near‑Eddington accretion or early massive seed formation, both of which remain debated.

## Why it matters
If supermassive black holes of this size existed at cosmic dawn, the mechanisms that feed them must operate far more efficiently than many simulations allow. This challenges the prevailing view that black holes grow primarily through gradual accretion and occasional mergers. The pair also provides a rare laboratory for studying how galaxy interactions influence black‑hole growth in the early universe, potentially reshaping our understanding of the first billion years of cosmic history.

## How to think about it
Treat this discovery as a boundary condition for any model of early black‑hole evolution. When building simulations, impose that at least a subset of halos must host >10⁸ M☉ black holes by 900 Myr, and explore mechanisms—such as direct‑collapse seeds or super‑Eddington accretion—that can achieve that growth. Use the pair’s separation to examine how close galaxy encounters can trigger rapid feeding, and consider follow‑up observations (e.g., ALMA, deeper JWST spectroscopy) to map the host galaxies’ gas reservoirs.

<Callout type="warning">Do not extrapolate the growth pathway of this single quasar pair to the entire early‑universe population without additional detections.</Callout>
