The BepiColombo mission, a joint ESA/JAXA venture to Mercury, has finally opened its eyes to the cosmos after an eight‑year cruise. Its Mercury Imaging X‑ray Spectrometer (MIXS) captured its first astronomical images, targeting the Crab Nebula, one of the brightest X‑ray sources in the sky. These observations let engineers compare the instrument’s on‑orbit performance with ground‑based calibrations from 2015. With Mercury’s orbital insertion slated for tomorrow, confirming MIXS’s health is a critical step. The results also demonstrate the durability of deep‑space hardware over decade‑long journeys.
<Callout type="takeaway">MIXS’s first X‑ray view confirms the instrument remains sharper than its original lab calibration, clearing a key hurdle before Mercury orbit.</Callout>
## What happened
After eight years traveling to the inner solar system, the BepiColombo spacecraft shed its Mercury Transfer Module in September, allowing the MIXS instrument to point freely at the sky for the first time. The team chose the Crab Nebula, a well‑characterized X‑ray beacon 6,500 light‑years away, to benchmark the spectrometer.
During September 11–12 the instrument performed 55 slews, accumulating about 39 hours of exposure. By measuring how the nebula’s image spreads across the detector, scientists derived an angular resolution that is marginally better than the 2015 laboratory measurement, indicating the optics have held up remarkably well.
Principal investigator Emma Bunce noted that these calibration data will feed directly into the surface composition maps that MIXS will generate once BepiColombo enters Mercury orbit.
## Why it matters
MIXS is the primary tool for mapping Mercury’s elemental makeup; its accuracy hinges on a well‑understood point‑spread function. Demonstrating that the instrument not only survived but improved its resolution boosts confidence in the mission’s scientific return. Moreover, the success validates the concept of long‑duration deep‑space payloads, informing the design of future interplanetary observatories.

## How to think about it
Treat the Crab Nebula observation as a baseline checkpoint. Compare future MIXS data against this reference to flag drift in resolution or sensitivity. For other missions, schedule similar early‑flight calibrations using well‑known celestial sources to verify instrument health before critical science phases.
<Callout type="warning">The modest improvement in angular resolution does not eliminate the risk of subtle degradations that could affect specific Mercury observations.</Callout>
