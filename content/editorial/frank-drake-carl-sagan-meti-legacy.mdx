The third installment of our Brief-ish History of METI picks up where the previous pieces left off, focusing on the partnership that turned scientific curiosity into cultural artifacts. After Frank Drake’s 1961 Green Bank symposium, he and Carl Sagan began a collaboration that caught NASA’s imagination. Their joint effort produced the Pioneer plaques and later the Voyager Golden Records, the first human messages designed to travel beyond the Solar System. These objects now drift through interstellar space, serving as time capsules of Earth’s story. Understanding their origin helps us see how scientific ambition can become a planetary legacy.

<Callout type="takeaway">Drake and Sagan’s collaboration turned abstract equations into tangible interstellar messages that still travel today.</Callout>

## What happened
In 1961 Drake organized an informal symposium at the Green Bank Observatory, shortly after his pioneering Project Ozma experiment. There he presented the equation that would bear his name, outlining the variables that determine the number of communicable extraterrestrial civilizations. Carl Sagan attended, was impressed, and the two began a long‑term partnership that blended rigorous science with public outreach.

Their collaboration attracted NASA’s support, leading to the design of the Pioneer plaques in the early 1970s. These aluminum discs, attached to the Pioneer 10 and 11 spacecraft, depicted a human figure and a map of our Solar System intended for any alien finder. A decade later, the Voyager program carried the Golden Records—gold‑plated phonograph discs containing music, greetings, and images of Earth—into interstellar space. Both projects remain the most iconic METI artifacts to date.

## Why it matters
The plaques and records illustrate a shift from passive listening (SETI) to active broadcasting (METI), raising philosophical and practical questions about humanity’s role as a signaler. They also demonstrate how scientific ideas can be packaged for cultural and diplomatic purposes, influencing public perception of space exploration. The legacy informs current debates about whether future generations should continue sending messages, and under what ethical frameworks.

## How to think about it
Treat each METI initiative as a policy decision rather than a purely scientific experiment. Evaluate the scientific merit, cultural impact, and risk profile before committing resources. A useful framework is to ask: (1) What is the message’s purpose? (2) Who decides its content? (3) What safeguards exist against unintended consequences? Applying this checklist can keep future projects grounded in both ambition and responsibility.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that sending a message guarantees contact; the vast distances and unknown receiver capabilities mean the odds of detection are extremely low.</Callout>
