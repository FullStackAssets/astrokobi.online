Margaret Hamilton, the software engineer who died at 90, helped land the first humans on the Moon. Her work on the Apollo Guidance Computer introduced concepts like asynchronous priority scheduling that rescued the 1969 landing when an overload threatened abort. The story illustrates how disciplined problem solving can turn limited hardware into reliable life‑support. Today her legacy informs safety‑critical software from autonomous vehicles to spacecraft.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hamilton’s disciplined problem‑solving turned fragile 1960s hardware into a reliable lunar landing system.</Callout>

## What happened
Born in 1936, Hamilton’s fascination with puzzles began in childhood, leading her to study mathematics and later join MIT’s Instrumentation Laboratory in the mid‑1960s.

At MIT she rose to director of software engineering for the Apollo Guidance Computer, where she wrote code that anticipated every conceivable fault, from sensor glitches to power spikes.

During Apollo 11’s descent, an unexpected program alarm overloaded the computer; Hamilton’s priority‑interrupt system automatically suspended non‑essential calculations, allowing the spacecraft to continue its landing sequence.

## Why it matters
Her techniques proved that software could compensate for hardware limits, a principle that underpins modern embedded systems. By demonstrating that rigorous error handling can save missions, Hamilton set a template for safety‑critical domains such as aviation, medical devices, and autonomous robotics. Moreover, her ascent in a male‑dominated field provides a powerful narrative for gender equity in engineering.

## How to think about it
When designing safety‑critical software, start by listing every possible failure mode and assign a priority that lets the system drop low‑importance tasks under stress. Pair this with extensive simulation of overload scenarios and maintain clear, versioned documentation. Encourage cross‑disciplinary curiosity, as Hamilton’s background in meteorology and astronomy enriched her problem‑solving toolkit.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat Hamilton’s ad‑hoc priority scheme as a substitute for modern formal verification; it should be integrated with systematic testing and certification processes.</Callout>
