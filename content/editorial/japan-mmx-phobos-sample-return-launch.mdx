On Oct. 19, 2026, Japan’s JAXA lifted the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) spacecraft aboard an H3 launch vehicle from Tanegashima. The mission will orbit Mars, land on Phobos, collect regolith, and return the material to Earth. A successful sample return would be the first from any moon of Mars and could resolve long‑standing questions about the moons’ origins and early solar‑system history.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX aims to be the first mission to land on and bring back samples from Phobos.</Callout>

## What happened
The H3 rocket lifted off from Pad‑2 at Tanegashima’s Yoshinobu Launch Complex at 3:41 p.m. EDT (19:41 GMT), a 207‑foot‑tall (63 m) two‑stage vehicle on its 10th flight. Four solid boosters separated in pairs just before the two‑minute mark, and the payload fairings were jettisoned about 1 min 20 s later, exposing MMX to space.

The first stage, powered by two LE‑9 engines burning liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, produced 331,000 lb (1,471 kN) of thrust. After main‑engine cut‑off at T+5 min, the second stage ignited for a 5.5‑minute burn, then coasted to a trans‑Mars injection burn roughly one hour and four minutes after liftoff. That burn lasted about six minutes and 25 seconds, after which MMX separated about one minute later, roughly 368 mi (592 km) above Earth.

Once on a trajectory toward Mars, MMX will enter orbit, perform a descent to Phobos, acquire up to a few hundred grams of surface material, and later launch a return capsule back to Earth for recovery.

## Why it matters
A Phobos sample would provide pristine material that predates the formation of the Martian moons, helping scientists test whether they are captured asteroids or debris from a giant impact. The data could refine models of early solar‑system dynamics and inform future crewed missions to Mars by revealing the composition of its satellite environment. Success also validates Japan’s new H3 launcher, positioning the country as a competitive player in deep‑space exploration.

## How to think about it
Treat MMX as a stepping stone: track its milestones, assess the performance of the H3 vehicle, and consider how the returned material could reshape theories of planetary formation. For policymakers, the mission highlights the value of international collaboration—JAXA is working with NASA and ESA on instruments and sample‑handling protocols. For enthusiasts, following the upcoming Phobos landing window (early 2028) offers a concrete way to gauge Japan’s deep‑space ambitions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the mission will succeed; sample‑return from a small, irregular moon remains technically challenging and could encounter unforeseen delays.</Callout>
