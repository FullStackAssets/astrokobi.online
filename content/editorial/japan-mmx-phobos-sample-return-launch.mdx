On Monday, October 19, 2026, Japan’s Martian Moons Exploration (MMX) spacecraft will lift off from Tanegashima aboard an H3 rocket. The mission’s primary goal is to orbit Phobos, land a small probe, and return soil and pebble samples to Earth. This will be the first time material from a Martian moon is brought back for laboratory analysis. Understanding Phobos’ composition could finally resolve how Mars acquired its moons and shed light on the early solar system.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX will deliver the first Phobos samples to Earth, opening a new window on Mars’ history.</Callout>

## What happened
The MMX spacecraft launches at 20:41 BST on an H3 carrier from the Tanegashima Space Center, beginning a five‑year mission that will take it to Mars’ larger moon, Phobos. After a cruise phase, the probe will enter orbit around Phobos, deploy a lander, and perform a touch‑down on the moon’s surface where it will collect soil and small pebbles using novel low‑gravity sampling tools.

The mission is a collaboration between JAXA, NASA, ESA, and the space agencies of France and Germany. It builds on JAXA’s heritage of asteroid sample return, notably the Hayabusa and Hayabusa 2 missions, adapting those techniques to Phobos, which has only about 0.1 % of Earth’s gravity.

Previous attempts to retrieve material from a Martian moon, such as Russia’s 2011 Phobos Grunt, failed before departure. MMX’s design incorporates lessons from that failure and from successful asteroid returns, aiming to avoid similar pitfalls.

## Why it matters
Phobos’ chemical makeup holds clues to whether the Martian moons are captured asteroids or the by‑product of a giant impact on Mars. Direct laboratory analysis can distinguish between these formation scenarios, refining models of planetary satellite formation across the solar system.

A successful sample return would also demonstrate that low‑gravity bodies can be accessed and studied, paving the way for future missions to Deimos, small asteroids, or even the icy moons of the outer planets. The international partnership showcases how shared expertise can tackle high‑risk, high‑reward science.

## How to think about it
Treat MMX as a prototype for the next generation of planetary sample‑return missions. Follow the mission’s milestones to gauge the maturity of low‑gravity extraction techniques, and consider how those methods could be adapted for future Deimos or asteroid missions. Keep an eye on the curation process: the returned material will undergo strict planetary‑protection protocols before scientists can access it, so timelines for data and publications will extend beyond the spacecraft’s return.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the samples will be publicly available immediately; they will be subject to controlled analysis and distribution.
</Callout>
