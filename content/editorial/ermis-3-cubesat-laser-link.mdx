On November 2, 2026, the Greek‑built ERMIS‑3 8U CubeSat succeeded in establishing a laser‑based optical link with the Holomondas Optical Ground Station in Thessaloniki. The link, achieved only weeks after the satellite’s commissioning, marks Greece’s first successful optical communication with a low‑Earth‑orbit satellite. By swapping radio‑frequency for a narrow laser beam, the demonstration shows how tiny satellites can transmit far more data in a short pass. This breakthrough could reshape how Earth‑observation constellations downlink high‑resolution imagery. The effort was supported by ESA and leveraged Astrolight’s ATLAS‑1 terminal.

<Callout type="takeaway">Optical laser links can give CubeSats data‑rates previously reserved for larger spacecraft.</Callout>

## What happened
ERMIS‑3, an 8U CubeSat developed by the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens, carried Astrolight’s ATLAS‑1 optical terminal. During a scheduled pass, the satellite acquired and tracked the Holomondas Optical Ground Station (HolOGS‑1) and exchanged data via a narrow laser beam. The demonstration validated the precise pointing, acquisition, and tracking (PAT) capability that the mission set out to prove.

The link was established using a ground‑based telescope equipped with a matching optical terminal, and the connection lasted for the duration of the pass, confirming that the system can operate under real orbital dynamics and atmospheric conditions. ESA contributed technical expertise, including environmental testing and optical alignment, to ensure the payload met the stringent requirements of space‑based laser communications.

## Why it matters
Optical communication offers orders‑of‑magnitude higher bandwidth than traditional radio‑frequency links, a critical advantage as Earth‑observation constellations generate ever‑larger volumes of high‑resolution data. Demonstrating this capability on a small, low‑cost CubeSat proves that high‑throughput downlinks are no longer exclusive to large, expensive platforms. The success also positions Greece as a new player in the emerging market for space‑based optical communications, potentially attracting commercial and research partnerships.

Moreover, the technology reduces spectrum congestion and can improve security, since laser beams are highly directional and difficult to intercept. As more missions adopt optical links, ground‑segment infrastructure will need to expand, but the payoff includes faster data delivery and new mission concepts that rely on real‑time high‑definition imagery.

## How to think about it
When evaluating whether to adopt optical communication for a CubeSat, start by mapping the mission’s data‑volume needs against the bandwidth limits of RF systems. If the required downlink exceeds those limits, consider the added mass, power, and cost of an optical terminal. Next, assess the ground‑segment environment: locations with clear skies and low turbulence maximize link availability. Finally, factor in operational complexity—precise PAT algorithms and robust fault‑tolerance are essential to maintain a link during the brief pass.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on optical downlinks when cloud cover or atmospheric turbulence is likely to obstruct the beam.</Callout>
