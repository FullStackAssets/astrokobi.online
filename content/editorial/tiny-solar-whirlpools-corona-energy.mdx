A new study using the Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope (DKIST) has identified tiny plasma whirlpools on the Sun’s surface that may solve the long‑standing coronal‑heating mystery. These vortices, only a few dozen kilometers across, appear in millions across the solar disk and each stores roughly 2 septillion ergs of kinetic energy. The research, led by Katariina Nykyri of Embry‑Riddle, calculates that the combined power of these whirlpools could match the energy required to keep the corona at millions of degrees. If correct, this mechanism would reshape our understanding of how magnetic and mechanical forces interact in stellar atmospheres.
<Callout type="takeaway">Tiny solar whirlpools may provide enough energy to heat the Sun’s corona.</Callout>
## What happened
The Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope captured ultra‑high‑resolution images of the photosphere, revealing swirling plasma structures just a few dozen kilometres in diameter. For the first time, these features—identified as Kelvin‑Helmholtz vortices—were resolved across the solar surface, confirming theoretical predictions of small‑scale turbulence.
Nykyri’s analysis applied the measured velocity shear and density contrast to estimate the kinetic energy of each vortex at about 2 septillion ergs. Multiplying by the estimated millions of vortices that form and dissipate every few minutes yields a total power comparable to the ~10^27 erg s⁻¹ needed to sustain the corona’s temperature.
The study, published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, argues that the continual conversion of vortex kinetic energy into magnetic stress could be the dominant heating channel.
## Why it matters
Resolving the coronal‑heating problem would close a gap in solar physics that has persisted for decades, improving models of solar wind acceleration and space‑weather forecasting. It also offers a template for how magnetic and mechanical energy interact in other stars, potentially influencing our broader understanding of stellar evolution. Finally, confirming this mechanism could guide the design of future solar missions aimed at capturing even finer plasma dynamics.

## How to think about it
Treat vortex heating as one component of a multi‑mechanism model: combine it with wave dissipation and reconnection processes when building coronal simulations. Use DKIST data to calibrate vortex frequency and energy, then test predictions against Solar Orbiter and Parker Solar Probe measurements. When evaluating solar activity forecasts, consider how changes in surface shear might modulate vortex production.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume vortex heating alone explains all coronal temperatures; uncertainties in vortex counts and energy conversion efficiency remain significant.</Callout>
