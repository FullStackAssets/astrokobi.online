The European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope has added a trove of early‑universe objects to the astronomical record. Its latest survey identified 31 quasars that existed when the cosmos was only about 670 million years old, including two that emitted as much light as a trillion suns. These quasars are powered by supermassive black holes that grew astonishingly fast in the universe’s first few hundred million years. Understanding how such massive engines formed so early reshapes theories of galaxy evolution and black‑hole growth.

<Callout type="takeaway">Euclid shows that luminous, supermassive black holes were already common when the universe was a fifth of its current age.</Callout>

## What happened
Euclid’s wide‑field imaging and near‑infrared spectroscopy allowed astronomers to scan a large swath of sky and pick out faint, high‑redshift sources that previous surveys missed. The team confirmed 31 quasars at redshifts corresponding to a universe only 5% of its present age, and two of them reached luminosities equivalent to a trillion solar outputs.

These record‑old quasars push the known frontier of black‑hole growth back by tens of millions of years. Their extreme brightness indicates that massive black holes of billions of solar masses were already in place, challenging models that struggle to assemble such mass so quickly after the Big Bang.

## Why it matters
The discovery expands the statistical sample of early quasars, moving beyond the handful of ultra‑luminous outliers that dominated earlier studies. A broader population lets researchers test competing scenarios for seed black‑hole formation, accretion efficiency, and the role of early galaxy mergers. Moreover, the presence of such energetic objects influences the ionization state of the surrounding intergalactic medium, affecting subsequent galaxy formation.

## How to think about it
When evaluating early‑universe discoveries, consider three layers: (1) observational reach—what the instrument can actually detect; (2) sample completeness—how many objects might be hidden below the detection threshold; and (3) theoretical context—whether existing models can accommodate the observed masses and luminosities. Applying this framework helps separate genuine challenges to theory from gaps that future deeper surveys can fill.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the reported luminosities directly translate to black‑hole masses without accounting for uncertainties in accretion efficiency and line‑of‑sight beaming.</Callout>
