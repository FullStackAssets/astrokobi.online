When we look at the Sun’s edge, we see countless spicules—thin, jet‑like eruptions that have long been thought of as individual, spinning plasma tornadoes. A recent study led by researchers from the University of Newcastle, the Indian Institute for Astrophysics and the University of Sheffield used the Pencil Code to simulate the Sun’s lower atmosphere in three dimensions. The simulation shows that what appear as separate spicules are actually the visual imprint of vast, folded magnetic sheets, or “curtains,” rippling through the plasma. If correct, this reinterpretation reshapes a key piece of the coronal heating puzzle and forces a rethink of how we model solar dynamics.

<Callout type="takeaway">Spicules are optical illusions of 3‑D magnetic curtains, not independent spinning jets.</Callout>

## What happened
The team constructed a high‑resolution magnetohydrodynamic model that spans from the convection zone beneath the solar surface up through the lower corona, using the open‑source Pencil Code. By injecting realistic acoustic forcing from granulation, the simulation generated magneto‑acoustic waves that travel along magnetic field lines, steepen into bow shocks, and interact with overlying magnetic structures.

Analysis of the resulting plasma dynamics revealed that the bright, narrow features traditionally identified as spicules are produced when folded magnetic sheets intersect the line of sight. The overlapping folds create the illusion of multiple, rapidly rotating jets, even though the underlying structure is a continuous, rippling curtain of plasma.

These findings were published without new spacecraft data; instead, the authors relied on the emergent behavior of the simulated environment to reinterpret decades of limb observations.

## Why it matters
If spicules are indeed manifestations of magnetic curtains, the energy transport mechanisms that feed the corona may be far more collective than previously thought. This could close part of the long‑standing coronal heating problem by linking granular acoustic energy directly to large‑scale magnetic reconfiguration. It also impacts space‑weather forecasting, as the dynamics of these curtains influence the release of mass and magnetic flux into the heliosphere.

## How to think about it
Treat limb observations as two‑dimensional projections of a three‑dimensional magnetic environment. When analyzing spicule imagery, ask whether the feature could arise from intersecting surfaces rather than isolated columns. Incorporate simulation‑driven expectations into data pipelines, and prioritize multi‑angle observations—such as those from Solar Orbiter’s out‑of‑ecliptic view—to discriminate between curtain and tornado geometries.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the curtain model replaces all existing spicule theories until observational evidence confirms it.</Callout>
