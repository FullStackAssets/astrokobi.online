The European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope has just delivered its first public data set, dubbed DR1‑Foundation. After 13 months of observations, the release covers 1900 square degrees—about 14 % of the sky Euclid will eventually survey—and contains more than one billion detected objects. By providing both imaging and spectroscopic measurements from the Wide and Deep surveys, the data give astronomers a first glimpse of the three‑dimensional cosmic web Euclid is designed to map. This milestone moves the mission from early commissioning to a science‑ready phase, and it sets the stage for the full 3‑D map that will probe dark energy and dark matter on the largest scales.

<Callout type="takeaway">DR1‑Foundation turns Euclid from a technology demonstrator into a data‑driven cosmology mission.</Callout>

## What happened
Euclid’s DR1‑Foundation combines data from the Wide Survey and the Deep Survey collected over a 13‑month period. The Wide Survey spans 1900 square degrees, imaging the sky in visible and near‑infrared bands while simultaneously obtaining low‑resolution spectra for millions of galaxies. The Deep Survey targets smaller, carefully chosen regions within the Wide footprint to reach fainter objects such as dwarf galaxies, low‑mass systems, and high‑redshift quasars.

Together the release records more than one billion sources, providing positions, photometry, and spectroscopic redshifts where available. Compared with ground‑based efforts like DESI, Euclid’s space‑based platform avoids atmospheric distortion, delivering higher angular resolution and more uniform coverage across its surveyed area.

## Why it matters
Mapping the large‑scale structure of the Universe is essential for constraining the properties of dark energy and dark matter, whose effects become apparent only over vast distances. Euclid’s early data already reveal the filamentary network of galaxy superclusters and the intervening voids, offering a preview of the statistical power the full mission will achieve. The combination of imaging and spectroscopy in a single space‑based survey provides a cleaner dataset than ground‑based projects, reducing systematic uncertainties tied to atmospheric seeing and absorption.

The release also enables cross‑correlation with existing surveys such as DESI, the Dark Energy Survey, and Gaia, sharpening cosmological parameter estimates and helping to calibrate systematic effects. While still covering just 14 % of the planned sky area, DR1‑Foundation establishes the processing pipelines and data quality standards needed for the mission’s ultimate goal: a three‑dimensional map of billions of galaxies spanning most of the observable Universe.

## How to think about it
Researchers should treat DR1‑Foundation as a foundation layer: use it to develop analysis methods, test cross‑survey correlations, and identify systematic effects before the full dataset arrives. When integrating Euclid data with ground‑based catalogs, prioritize common sky regions and apply consistent photometric calibrations to mitigate zero‑point offsets. Keep in mind the current sky coverage limits the statistical weight of any single‑field study; combine multiple fields where possible to approach the survey’s full statistical power. Finally, stay alert for upcoming data releases that will expand both area and depth, gradually refining cosmological measurements.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume DR1 alone can deliver definitive dark‑energy constraints; the limited sky fraction and early‑stage calibrations mean results should be regarded as provisional.</Callout>
