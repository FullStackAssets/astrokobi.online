NASA’s Safe and Precise Landing—Integrated Capabilities Evolution (SPLICE) payload guided an Alta‑X drone through a simulated lunar descent on Aug. 27, demonstrating autonomous terrain mapping and safe‑site selection. The test, conducted at Rogers Dry Lake near the Armstrong Flight Research Center, showed the system can generate high‑resolution digital elevation maps and divert the vehicle if the target area proves hazardous. Success marks a key step toward fully autonomous landings on the Moon and other planetary bodies, reducing dependence on ground‑based commands.

<Callout type="takeaway">SPLICE proved it can autonomously identify and navigate to safe landing zones during a realistic lunar descent simulation.</Callout>

## What happened
The SPLICE payload, developed at NASA’s Johnson Space Center, was integrated onto an Alta‑X drone and flown over Rogers Dry Lake on Aug. 27, 2026. During the flight the guidance system scanned the terrain with lidar, produced a high‑resolution digital elevation map, and evaluated potential touchdown sites in real time. When the algorithm flagged the intended spot as unsafe, the drone automatically altered its trajectory to a safer location and completed a soft touchdown simulation.

Earlier in July, on the 15th, the same configuration performed a series of descent maneuvers that verified communications, telemetry, and command‑and‑control links between the payload and the aircraft. NASA engineers used the data to refine the navigation software and plan a full‑scale test at Voyager Technologies in Mojave later this month.

## Why it matters
Autonomous landing reduces the need for continuous ground intervention, a critical advantage for missions where communication delays are significant, such as Mars or the far side of the Moon. By proving that a spacecraft can assess surface hazards and re‑target in flight, SPLICE paves the way for more flexible mission designs, higher payload margins, and the ability to land in scientifically valuable but rugged terrain. The technology also supports future commercial and international lunar initiatives that require reliable, repeatable soft‑landing capabilities.

## How to think about it
When evaluating autonomous landing tech, weigh the maturity of sensor fusion against the mission’s risk tolerance. For near‑term lunar missions, pair SPLICE‑style guidance with redundant inertial navigation to mitigate sensor blind spots. For longer‑duration deep‑space landers, incorporate extensive on‑board validation runs that emulate dust, low‑gravity, and thermal extremes before committing to a final descent sequence.

<Callout type="warning">The system has only been validated in simulated descent; real lunar surface conditions could present unforeseen challenges.</Callout>
