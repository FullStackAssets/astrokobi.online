Capotauro appeared in James Webb Space Telescope infrared images as a faint red source that seemed to match the spectral cutoff expected from a galaxy at redshift ≈ 32, roughly 100 million years after the Big Bang. Follow‑up imaging taken about 3½ years later showed the object had moved, a clear sign of proximity within the Milky Way. The shift reclassifies Capotauro as a cold Y‑type brown dwarf rather than a primordial galaxy. This correction reshapes expectations for the earliest observable galaxies and highlights the diagnostic power of proper‑motion measurements. It also adds a new member to the census of nearby ultracool brown dwarfs.

<Callout type="takeaway">Proper motion proved that Capotauro is a nearby brown dwarf, not an ancient galaxy.</Callout>

## What happened
The object, nicknamed Capotauro, was first reported in February as a candidate galaxy whose infrared colors suggested a redshift of about 32. Its spectrum showed a sharp rise at longer wavelengths, a feature that can also arise from water, methane, and ammonia absorption in Y‑type brown dwarfs. When Webb revisited the field after roughly 3.5 years, the source had shifted position relative to distant background galaxies.

The measured motion placed Capotauro at a distance consistent with the Milky Way, ruling out the high‑redshift interpretation. The research team, led by F.-Y. F. Liu of the University of Edinburgh, published the revised classification in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.

## Why it matters
The discovery underscores that photometric redshifts alone can be misleading, especially for objects with extreme infrared colors. It forces a reassessment of candidate ultra‑high‑redshift galaxies identified solely by color cuts, potentially lowering early‑galaxy counts. At the same time, it enriches the sample of nearby cold brown dwarfs, offering fresh laboratories for atmospheric chemistry and substellar evolution.

## How to think about it
When evaluating putative high‑redshift objects, treat photometric color cuts as a first filter, not a final verdict. Cross‑check with proper‑motion data whenever multiple epochs exist, and consider spectroscopic follow‑up to resolve ambiguous spectral features. For brown dwarf hunters, prioritize objects that show steep infrared rises combined with measurable motion to separate them from distant galaxies.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume an extreme redshift solely from infrared color cuts without corroborating motion or spectroscopic evidence.</Callout>
