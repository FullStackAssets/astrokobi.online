When rockets touch down on the Moon, the plume blasts up a cloud of abrasive regolith that can damage hardware and endanger crews. A team of undergraduate and graduate engineers at the University of Alabama in Huntsville has built and tested a 12‑foot‑wide prototype landing pad that vents exhaust and traps dust. The pad’s interlocking concrete modules were subjected to three rocket‑motor firings at the university’s Propulsion Research Center this summer. Demonstrating that a vented, modular design can survive lunar‑style thrust is a concrete step toward safer Artemis landings.

<Callout type="takeaway">A vented, modular landing pad can mitigate lunar dust hazards while protecting both spacecraft and surface infrastructure.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s Artemis program has funded a Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) partnership that pairs the university’s Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering department with Linc Research Inc. The collaboration tasked the student team, led by Dr. Judy Schneider, with creating a scalable landing‑pad concept that could be manufactured from lunar‑derived materials.

The team fabricated individual concrete tiles, each designed to interlock and contain vent channels that redirect rocket plume away from the pad surface. Assembled into a 3.65 m (12 ft) diameter prototype, the pad was mounted on a test stand and exposed to three short‑duration rocket‑motor firings. Sensors recorded that the vent system captured over 80 % of the exhaust flow and that surface temperatures remained below the concrete’s cracking threshold.

## Why it matters
If Artemis and future commercial missions can land repeatedly without coating vehicles in abrasive dust, hardware longevity improves and crew health risks drop. A reusable, dust‑mitigating pad also reduces the mass that must be launched from Earth, because later pads could be built from in‑situ resources. The technology therefore supports lower‑cost, higher‑frequency lunar operations and paves the way for sustained surface habitats.

## How to think about it
Treat the vented pad as a modular subsystem rather than a monolithic platform. First, evaluate the local regolith composition to choose an ISRU‑compatible material. Next, model plume‑dust interactions in vacuum to refine vent geometry before committing to flight hardware. Finally, integrate the pad design with mission architecture early, so launch‑mass budgets can account for the added but reusable infrastructure.

<Callout type="warning">Earth‑gravity and atmospheric pressure tests do not capture the full behavior of lunar plumes; further vacuum‑chamber testing is required before flight qualification.</Callout>
