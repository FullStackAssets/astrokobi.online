An unclassified Defense Intelligence Agency assessment released this week includes the first high‑resolution view of a Chinese winged spaceplane orbiting Earth. The image, captured by a Vantor (formerly Maxar) commercial imaging satellite in 2024, shows a vehicle with delta wings, payload bay doors, a dark heat shield and a rear‑mounted solar‑array service module. Its silhouette closely mirrors the U.S. Air Force’s X‑37B, suggesting a convergent design path for reusable space assets. The appearance of a secret Chinese spaceplane in low‑Earth orbit raises fresh questions about its mission set and strategic intent.

<Callout type="takeaway">China’s winged spaceplane in LEO signals a new, opaque capability that could reshape space security.</Callout>

## What happened
The DIA report includes a clear photo taken by Vantor’s Maxar‑derived imaging satellite, revealing delta wings, payload bay doors, a dark heat shield and a rear‑mounted service module with solar arrays. The vehicle’s overall shape is a strong visual match to the U.S. X‑37B, the only known reusable spaceplane in operation.

Publicly available lower‑resolution views had hinted at the craft, but the DIA assessment released the full‑resolution image for the first time. The report notes that China has launched four spaceplane missions since 2020, each lasting up to nine months, with the fourth mission launched in February and still in orbit.

Like the X‑37B, the Chinese craft lifts off inside a conventional rocket’s payload shroud, operates as a satellite, and returns autonomously for a runway landing. The U.S. Space Force currently flies two X‑37Bs on a rotation, with the longest mission to date lasting two‑and‑a‑half years.

## Why it matters
The presence of a reusable, winged platform expands China’s on‑orbit capabilities beyond traditional satellites, potentially enabling rapid experiment cycles, on‑orbit servicing, or even the testing of directed‑energy concepts noted in the DIA threat assessment. For the United States, the opaque nature of the missions adds strategic uncertainty and could complicate space situational awareness. The broader implication is a possible acceleration of a space‑based arms race, where reusable vehicles become testbeds for capabilities that were previously limited to ground‑based platforms.

## How to think about it
Treat the Chinese spaceplane as a new class of on‑orbit asset that blends satellite and spacecraft functions. Track its orbital parameters with commercial SSA data, compare mission durations to known X‑37B cycles, and assess any changes in launch cadence. Incorporate the observations into a risk‑assessment framework that weighs technological parity, potential weaponization pathways, and debris mitigation. Use the insights to inform policy discussions on transparency measures and norms for reusable space platforms.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the vehicle is currently armed with directed‑energy weapons; the DIA assessment notes only the possibility of future weaponization.</Callout>
