NASA has opened the next round of astronaut applications, targeting candidates who can live and work on a future lunar base. The call, announced at a Kennedy Space Center air show, sets a Dec 3 deadline for U.S. citizens with advanced STEM degrees or piloting experience. Selected astronauts will help shape the Artemis program’s push toward a permanent Moon presence and the eventual crewed missions to Mars. This marks the agency’s first recruitment drive since 2024, when it accepted just ten out of more than 8,000 applicants. The new cohort could be the first to spend extended periods on the Moon.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s new astronaut recruitment aims to build a lunar workforce that will pave the way for Mars exploration.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA announced at the Kennedy Space Center air show that applications for the next astronaut class are open, with a submission deadline of 3 December. Eligible candidates must be U.S. citizens and hold a master’s degree in a STEM field, medical qualifications, or pilot training, plus at least three years of relevant professional experience and specific flight‑hour thresholds for pilots. The agency expects to receive thousands of applications and will narrow the pool over the coming months.

The selection process will culminate in a final decision in late 2027, after which the new astronauts will begin intensive training for lunar surface operations, habitat construction, and eventual Mars transit. The last recruitment cycle in 2024 yielded ten new astronauts from over 8,000 applicants, while the current corps includes 32 active members.

These hires are part of the Artemis program’s goal to return humans to the Moon by 2028 and to establish a sustainable presence that supports scientific research and commercial activities, with partners such as SpaceX and Blue Origin slated to provide launch services.

## Why it matters
A dedicated lunar workforce transforms the Moon from a short‑term destination into a long‑term platform for science, industry, and a stepping stone to Mars. By selecting astronauts with diverse technical backgrounds, NASA aims to accelerate habitat development, in‑situ resource utilization, and the testing of life‑support systems needed for deep‑space travel. The program also signals continued U.S. leadership in space, influencing international cooperation and commercial investment.

## How to think about it
If you are considering applying, map your existing qualifications against NASA’s criteria and identify any gaps—such as additional flight hours or advanced research experience—and address them through targeted training or graduate study. For policymakers, view the recruitment as a metric of program health: a robust, diverse applicant pool can justify continued funding and international partnerships. Finally, observers should treat the announced timeline as a flexible roadmap; technical challenges or budget shifts could adjust dates, so stay updated on Artemis milestones.
