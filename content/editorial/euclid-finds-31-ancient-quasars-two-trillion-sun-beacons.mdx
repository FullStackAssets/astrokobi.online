The European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope has added 31 quasars to the catalog of the early universe, including two that emit as much light as a trillion Suns. These objects existed when the cosmos was only about 670 million years old, roughly 5 % of its current age. Their discovery expands the known population of ancient quasars beyond the handful of ultra‑bright examples previously identified. By probing fainter sources, Euclid provides a more complete view of how the first supermassive black holes grew. Understanding this growth is essential for models of galaxy formation.

<Callout type="takeaway">Euclid’s survey reveals a hidden, diverse population of early quasars, showing that trillion‑sun beacons were not isolated anomalies.</Callout>

## What happened
Euclid, launched in 2023, surveyed vast swaths of the sky with infrared and optical instruments designed to detect faint, distant objects. The mission’s latest data release lists 31 quasars that formed when the universe was only about 5 % of its present age, including two that shine with a luminosity comparable to a trillion Suns.

Quasars are powered by supermassive black holes accreting gas at extreme rates, releasing prodigious energy that outshines their host galaxies. Detecting them at such early times is difficult because their light is both faint and redshifted, and they can be confused with nearer stars. Euclid’s combination of deep imaging and precise photometric redshifts allowed astronomers to separate these ancient beacons from foreground objects and to identify fainter examples that previous surveys missed.

## Why it matters
The expanded sample challenges the view that only the most luminous, rare quasars existed in the first billion years. It suggests that supermassive black holes could grow rapidly in a broader range of environments, influencing early galaxy formation and the reionization of the intergalactic medium. The findings also validate Euclid’s capability to contribute to extragalactic archaeology, complementing its primary dark‑energy mission.

## How to think about it
Researchers should incorporate these quasars into population synthesis models, testing scenarios that produce rapid black‑hole growth without overproducing luminous sources. Observers can prioritize follow‑up spectroscopy to refine redshift and black‑hole mass measurements, reducing current uncertainties. For educators and enthusiasts, the discovery illustrates how modern surveys can rewrite narratives about the universe’s infancy, emphasizing that early cosmic structures were more varied than previously thought.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the two trillion‑sun quasars represent the typical brightness of all early quasars; they remain extreme outliers within a still‑incomplete sample.</Callout>
