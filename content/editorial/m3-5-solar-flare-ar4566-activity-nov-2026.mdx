After a week of only weak B‑class flares, the Sun woke up on November 5, 2026 with a moderate M3.5 eruption from the magnetically complex region AR4566. The flare peaked at 18:33 UTC and was accompanied by a minor R1 radio blackout over the Pacific. It also launched a coronal mass ejection, though early modeling suggests the CME is not Earth‑directed. This burst of activity lifted the daily flare count to 13, the highest in several days. The event re‑establishes moderate solar conditions after a period of quiet.

<Callout type="takeaway">The M3.5 flare from AR4566 signals a return to moderate solar activity.</Callout>

## What happened
The active region AR4566, classified beta‑gamma‑delta for its tangled magnetic field, produced an M3.5 flare that reached its maximum at 18:33 UTC on November 5. The event generated a minor R1 radio blackout over the Pacific Ocean, off the coast of Peru, and expelled a coronal mass ejection (CME) into interplanetary space.

In the 24‑hour window surrounding the flare, the Sun emitted a total of 13 flares: one M‑class, eleven C‑class, and one B‑class. Newcomer region AR4568 dominated the C‑class activity with seven flares, while AR4566 contributed the M‑class event plus four additional C‑class flares. Five numbered regions were visible on the Earth‑facing side, but only AR4566 and AR4568 showed measurable activity.

## Why it matters
Moderate flares like M3.5 can disrupt high‑frequency radio communications and increase radiation exposure for satellites and high‑altitude flights. The accompanying CME, even if not Earth‑directed, serves as a reminder that solar storms can evolve quickly, prompting space‑weather forecasters to update models and issue alerts. Industries reliant on GPS, power‑grid stability, and satellite operations must monitor such events closely.

## How to think about it
Treat the flare as a signal that the Sun’s activity cycle is shifting back toward moderate levels. Track real‑time space‑weather dashboards for CME trajectory updates, and adjust satellite maneuver plans only after a clear Earth‑impact forecast. For ground‑based systems, schedule critical HF‑dependent operations outside the predicted blackout window and have backup communication channels ready.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the CME will cause geomagnetic storms without confirmed Earth‑directed trajectory; premature protective actions can waste resources.</Callout>
