The Biological and Physical Sciences Division at NASA has launched the BPS Digital AVATAR Challenge, inviting researchers to build computational twins of the bone‑marrow organ‑on‑a‑chip that flew on Artemis II. The original AVATAR experiment returned crew‑derived cells after a 10‑day lunar flyby, creating the first personalized tissue analog from an astronaut. By turning those data into a digital twin, NASA hopes to predict how each crew member’s immune and hematopoietic systems will react to microgravity, radiation, and other space stressors. Success could mean real‑time, individualized medical strategies for missions to Mars and beyond.

<Callout type="takeaway">Personalized digital twins could become the cornerstone of astronaut medical care on long‑duration missions.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s BPS division announced the challenge in November 2026, outlining requirements for models that ingest multi‑modal datasets—omics, physiological readouts, and environmental parameters—to simulate bone‑marrow responses. The call emphasizes modular architectures that can be updated as new flight data arrive, and it seeks both proof‑of‑concept simulations and scalable pipelines for future crew members.

The AVATAR hardware, a microfluidic chip seeded with astronaut‑derived stem cells, was the first to demonstrate a “virtual astronaut” concept in orbit. Data from the Artemis II flight include gene‑expression shifts, cytokine profiles, and cellular proliferation rates under lunar‑flight conditions. Participants must translate these measurements into a computational framework that can differentiate individual‑specific effects from crew‑wide trends.

Proposals are evaluated on scientific rigor, integration of established computational biology methods, and the ability to generate actionable health predictions—such as risk of anemia or immune suppression—during a mission. The challenge runs through mid‑2027, with NASA planning to pilot the most promising twins on upcoming Artemis and deep‑space flights.

## Why it matters
Crew health is the limiting factor for missions beyond low Earth orbit; radiation and microgravity can compromise bone marrow function, leading to anemia, immune deficits, and impaired wound healing. A digital twin that predicts these outcomes for each astronaut enables pre‑emptive countermeasures, reducing medical emergencies and mission aborts. Beyond space, the technology could accelerate personalized medicine on Earth by providing a testbed for organ‑on‑a‑chip modeling.

## How to think about it
1. **Data consolidation** – Gather multi‑omics, physiological, and environmental streams into a unified schema. 2. **Modular modeling** – Build separate sub‑models for hematopoiesis, immune signaling, and microgravity‑induced stress, then couple them. 3. **Iterative validation** – Compare twin outputs against ground‑based analog studies and any available in‑flight biomarkers, refining parameters continuously. 4. **Decision integration** – Embed twin forecasts into mission medical planning tools, allowing crew doctors to weigh countermeasure options in real time.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely solely on twin predictions for clinical decisions until the models are rigorously validated against in‑flight data.</Callout>
