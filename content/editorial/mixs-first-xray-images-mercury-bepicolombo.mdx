After an eight‑year cruise through interplanetary space, the Mercury Imaging X‑ray Spectrometer (MIXS) aboard ESA/JAXA’s BepiColombo finally opened its eyes to the cosmos. In September, the instrument performed a 39‑hour observation of the Crab Nebula, executing 55 slews to map the source. The data reveal an angular resolution that slightly exceeds the 2015 laboratory baseline. These first images arrive just before the spacecraft’s scheduled orbital insertion around Mercury on Nov 21, offering a timely performance check.

<Callout type="takeaway">MIXS’s first X‑ray images confirm the instrument is performing better than expected, clearing a key hurdle before Mercury orbit.</Callout>

## What happened

The MIXS instrument, built by the University of Leicester, has been traveling with BepiColombo’s Mercury Transfer Module since launch. After the MTM separated from the Mercury Planetary Orbiter and the Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter in early September, the spectrometer was finally able to point toward deep space for the first time.

Between 11 and 12 September, the team commanded 55 spacecraft slews, accumulating 39 hours of exposure on the Crab Nebula, a well‑characterized X‑ray source 6,500 light‑years away. By measuring how the nebula’s image spreads across the detector, scientists derived an angular resolution marginally sharper than the pre‑flight measurement made in 2015.

## Why it matters

The performance check is critical because MIXS will later map elemental composition on Mercury’s surface using reflected solar X‑rays. A sharper resolution improves the ability to distinguish geological features and to separate signal from background noise. Successful calibration also validates the long‑duration reliability of hardware that has endured eight years in the harsh space environment, influencing design choices for future deep‑space missions.

## How to think about it

Treat these results as a baseline rather than a final performance metric. Follow the upcoming BepiColombo data releases to see how MIXS behaves in Mercury’s magnetosphere and on the planet’s day side. When evaluating future mission concepts, consider the demonstrated durability of MIXS as evidence that complex instruments can survive long cruise phases without degradation.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the current angular resolution will remain unchanged once MIXS operates in Mercury’s high‑temperature, high‑radiation environment.</Callout>
