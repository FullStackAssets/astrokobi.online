The fossil that sparked a new dinosaur discovery spent a century hidden in a museum drawer. Amateur paleontologist Reginald Walter Tooley collected the bones on the Isle of Wight more than 100 years ago, and they arrived at the Natural History Museum in 1924 among roughly a thousand unstudied specimens. In 2026, UCL paleontologists Cassius Morrison and Charlie Scherer used CT scanning and 3‑D modeling to re‑examine the specimen, identifying it as a distinct megaraptoran they named Invictibellatrix amberina. The find reshapes our view of mid‑Cretaceous predators in Europe and shows how modern imaging can revive forgotten collections.

<Callout type="takeaway">A century‑old, overlooked fossil has revealed a new feathered megaraptoran, proving that museum backlogs still hold major discoveries.</Callout>

## What happened
Two paleontologists from University College London pulled the long‑overlooked specimen from storage and subjected it to high‑resolution CT scanning. The scans exposed internal bone structures that were invisible to the naked eye, allowing the team to reconstruct a detailed 3‑D model of the animal.

Using the model, they identified a distinctive hour‑glass‑shaped crest and a unique combination of limb proportions that did not match any known species. The researchers published their findings in Royal Society Open Science on October 14, 2026, formally naming the new species Invictibellatrix amberina, a nod to the amber‑rich deposits of the Wessex Formation and the Latin for “unconquered female warrior.”

## Why it matters
The discovery adds the first known European megaraptoran, expanding the geographic range of this predatory clade and prompting a reassessment of Cretaceous ecosystem dynamics on the Isle of Wight. It also highlights the scientific value of museum collections that remain unstudied, suggesting that many more taxa may be waiting in drawers. Finally, the successful use of CT scanning and digital modeling demonstrates how non‑destructive techniques can extract new data from old fossils, setting a precedent for future re‑examinations.

## How to think about it
Treat this find as a proof‑of‑concept rather than a definitive portrait of a new lineage. When encountering a solitary specimen, combine imaging results with comparative anatomy from better‑known relatives, and remain open to revision as additional material emerges. Institutions should prioritize systematic digitization of backlogged fossils, but also allocate funding for targeted field surveys that can validate museum‑based hypotheses.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the full biology, behavior, or variation of Invictibellatrix amberina from this single partial skeleton; further discoveries are needed to confirm its traits.</Callout>
