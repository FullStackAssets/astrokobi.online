On the morning of November 3, 2026, the Moon slipped in front of Jupiter, briefly hiding the planet from view. Observers in parts of Asia, Australia and New Zealand reported the occultation, and photographer Michael Teoh in Malaysia captured the event along with two of Jupiter’s moons. A second occultation will occur on November 30 for southern‑hemisphere locations such as southern Argentina and Chile. These moments are part of a series of November sky alignments that also feature a close Jupiter‑Mars conjunction and the Leonid meteor shower peak.

## What happened
On November 3 2026 the Moon passed directly in front of Jupiter, an event known as a lunar occultation. The alignment was visible to the naked eye for a few seconds in regions of Asia, Australia and New Zealand, and Michael Teoh in Malaysia recorded the planet and two of its moons. The occultation will repeat on November 30, when observers in southern South America will see the Moon cover Jupiter again.

Throughout November Jupiter rises late at night and stays visible until sunrise, moving closer to Mars and the bright star Regulus in Leo. The Jupiter‑Mars conjunction reaches its closest approach on November 15, with the planets separated by just over two Moon‑widths. The Leonid meteor shower peaks on November 17‑18, radiating from a point near the same part of the sky, adding a meteor backdrop to the planetary pairings.

## Why it matters
These alignments turn the night sky into a natural classroom, letting the public witness planetary motion in real time and inspiring amateur imaging projects. Astronomers can use occultation timings to refine lunar limb profiles, while the proximity of Jupiter and Mars offers a chance to compare their apparent motions against orbital models. The concurrent Leonids add a meteor component that can boost public interest in both planetary and meteoric phenomena.

## How to think about it
First, check local rise times for the Moon and Jupiter on the target date using a planet‑watching app. If you are in a suitable region, find a dark site with a clear horizon and use binoculars or a small telescope to enhance the view; the Moon’s limb will silhouette Jupiter for a brief moment. Record the exact time of disappearance and re‑appearance if you can—those timestamps contribute to citizen‑science databases. Finally, plan to stay after the occultation to watch the Leonids or the Jupiter‑Mars conjunction, which follow within the same week.
