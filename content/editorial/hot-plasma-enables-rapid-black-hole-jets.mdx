A long‑standing puzzle in astrophysics is how supermassive black holes that gulp down matter at extreme rates can still power bright, relativistic jets. A team led by Hirofumi Noda and Kazuhiro Hada combined the new X‑ray Imaging and Spectroscopy Mission (XRISM) data with high‑resolution radio and millimeter observations of the active galaxy 3C 120. Their analysis shows that the cold accretion disk truncates at about 20 gravitational radii, giving way to a scorching plasma flow that appears to launch the jet. This finding links the hot inner flow directly to jet formation, reshaping theories of black‑hole feeding and feedback.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hot plasma inside the truncated disk provides the launchpad for powerful jets in rapidly accreting black holes.</Callout>

## What happened
The XRISM spectrometer detected a broadened iron K‑α emission line from 3C 120’s accretion disk. Relativistic distortion of the line indicated that the optically thick, cool disk ends roughly 20 R₉ from the event horizon, beyond which the emission profile changes.

Simultaneous very‑long‑baseline interferometry with the Global Millimeter VLBI Array, the VLBA, and the East Asian VLBI Network mapped the base of the radio jet to within a few gravitational radii. The jet’s narrowing point coincides with the region where the hot, tenuous plasma—heated to hundreds of millions of degrees—dominates the flow.

The results, published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, present the first coordinated X‑ray and radio view that directly connects the hot inner flow to the jet’s origin.

## Why it matters
By demonstrating that a hot, radiatively inefficient plasma can replace the inner cold disk, the study challenges models that require a thin disk to collimate jets. It also offers a concrete mechanism for how rapidly accreting black holes can inject energy into their host galaxies, influencing star formation and galaxy evolution. Finally, the work validates XRISM as a tool for probing the extreme environments where gravity, magnetism, and plasma physics intersect.

## How to think about it
When evaluating jet production in active galactic nuclei, first assess whether the accretion disk shows signs of truncation near the black hole. If a hot, optically thin plasma is present, consider magnetohydrodynamic processes—such as the Blandford‑Znajek or Blandford‑Payne mechanisms—as likely drivers of the jet. Incorporate multi‑band data (X‑ray spectroscopy for disk structure, high‑resolution radio imaging for jet base) into simulations to test whether the observed hot flow can supply the required magnetic flux and pressure.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that every rapidly feeding black hole launches jets via the same hot‑flow mechanism without additional observational confirmation.</Callout>
