Four years after NASA’s DART mission deliberately struck Dimorphos, Europe’s Hera spacecraft is now skimming the scarred moonlet at just 25‑30 km distance. From this close range Hera can map the impact crater, sample the debris cloud, and test technologies for future planetary‑defense missions. The observations will reveal how a kinetic impact reshapes a small body’s orbit and surface, data that could be decisive if Earth ever faces a similar threat.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hera’s close‑up survey will turn the DART test site into a laboratory for planetary‑defense science.</Callout>

## What happened
ESA announced that Hera is currently orbiting Dimorphos and its parent asteroid Didymos from 15‑18 miles (25‑30 km) away. The spacecraft, launched in 2024, carries 25 science instruments and two Cubesats designed to image the crater, measure the altered orbit, and search for lingering debris.

Next month Hera will descend to about 12 miles (20 km) altitude, the closest approach planned for the mission. The DART impact in 2022 shortened Dimorphos’s orbital period around Didymos and nudged the moonlet onto a slightly different solar trajectory, while ejecting rubble that has formed a debris trail extending more than 10,000 km.

## Why it matters
Understanding how a kinetic impact changes an asteroid’s trajectory is the cornerstone of planetary‑defense strategy. Hera’s measurements of the crater morphology, momentum transfer, and debris distribution will validate models that predict the effectiveness of future deflection attempts. The data also inform spacecraft navigation safety, since lingering fragments could endanger later missions to the same system.

## How to think about it
Treat Hera’s findings as a case study rather than a universal rulebook. Compare the measured momentum boost with simulation outputs to gauge model fidelity, and consider the debris environment when planning any follow‑up missions. Policy makers should weigh the demonstrated capability against the cost and risk of deploying similar kinetic‑impact systems for Earth‑bound threats.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the vicinity of Dimorphos is free of debris; future spacecraft must plan trajectories that avoid the extended debris field.</Callout>
