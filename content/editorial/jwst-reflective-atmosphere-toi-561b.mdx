The ultra‑short‑period exoplanet TOI‑561b, once thought to be a barren lava world, has surprised astronomers with a highly reflective atmosphere revealed by JWST/NIRSpec phase‑curve observations. The planet orbits its star in just 0.45 days, exposing its surface to extreme stellar radiation that should strip any volatile envelope. Yet the new data show the planet reflects more than 70 % of incoming starlight, implying the presence of reflective clouds or a silicate‑vapor atmosphere. This finding challenges the prevailing view that USP planets are airless and offers a rare glimpse into secondary atmospheric processes on rocky worlds. Understanding TOI‑561b’s atmosphere could reshape models of planetary evolution and habitability.

<Callout type="takeaway">TOI‑561b’s bright, reflective atmosphere suggests that even the hottest ultra‑short‑period planets can retain secondary envelopes.</Callout>

## What happened
JWST’s NIRSpec instrument recorded phase‑curve photometry of TOI‑561b across multiple infrared bands, measuring the planet’s brightness as it orbited its star. The light curve exhibited a pronounced secondary eclipse and a high dayside flux, leading to an inferred geometric albedo of roughly 0.7 – far higher than any known rocky exoplanet.

The results were published in *The Astronomical Journal* under the title “A Highly Reflective Atmosphere on the Lava World TOI‑561b Revealed by JWST/NIRSpec Phase Curves.” Lead author Samuel Boucher and collaborators argue that the most plausible explanation is a thin, silicate‑rich vapor atmosphere that condenses into reflective clouds on the planet’s scorching dayside.

## Why it matters
If a USP planet can sustain a secondary atmosphere, existing models of rapid atmospheric loss under intense stellar irradiation must be revised. The presence of reflective clouds also hints at active surface‑atmosphere exchange, offering a natural laboratory for studying magma‑atmosphere interactions. Moreover, the detection validates phase‑curve spectroscopy as a powerful tool for probing atmospheres on planets that are otherwise too hot for traditional transmission spectroscopy.

## How to think about it
Treat TOI‑561b as a test case: first assess the measured albedo against theoretical limits for bare lava surfaces; if the reflectivity exceeds those limits, an atmospheric layer becomes the favored explanation. Next, compare the phase‑curve spectrum to models of silicate‑vapor clouds versus molten rock emission. Finally, prioritize follow‑up observations—such as JWST emission spectroscopy at longer wavelengths—to search for molecular signatures that can confirm a gaseous envelope.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the high reflectivity automatically proves an atmosphere; additional spectroscopic data are needed to rule out a purely reflective lava surface.</Callout>
