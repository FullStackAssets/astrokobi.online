On October 29, 2026 the Defense Intelligence Agency published a new satellite photograph that captures China’s Shenlong space plane physically attached to another spacecraft. The image, taken by Vantor’s WorldView‑3 sensor in 2024 from roughly 56 miles (90 km) altitude, resolves features as small as 4.5 cm per pixel, revealing open payload‑bay doors and the docking configuration. This is the first time the secretive vehicle has been seen docked in orbit, offering a rare glimpse into its on‑board operations. The visual evidence raises fresh questions about China’s on‑orbit capabilities and the strategic balance in space.

<Callout type="takeaway">Shenlong’s docking image shows China is testing on‑orbit servicing and rendezvous that could reshape space logistics.</Callout>

## What happened
The DIA report includes a high‑resolution photograph taken by the WorldView‑3 satellite that shows Shenlong attached to an unidentified satellite with solar panels deployed. The photo also captures Shenlong’s payload‑bay doors in an open position, a detail previously only inferred from ground‑based sightings.

Observers note that this docking occurred during Shenlong’s fourth mission, which launched in February 2026. Earlier missions in 2023 and 2024 released objects and performed proximity operations, but none were captured in such clear detail. In June 2026, LeoLabs independently cataloged an object they attributed to Shenlong, confirming the vehicle’s ability to deploy sub‑satellites.

## Why it matters
The image confirms that China can not only launch a reusable space plane but also conduct on‑orbit docking, a capability that underpins satellite servicing, refueling, and assembly of larger structures. Such operations expand the strategic utility of a single launch, potentially lowering costs for future missions and altering the calculus of space‑based power projection. Allies and competitors alike must reassess threat models that previously assumed China’s space activities were limited to launch and retrieval.

## How to think about it
Treat the photo as a data point, not a definitive statement of intent. Track subsequent DIA releases, commercial imaging, and ground‑based radar to see whether the unknown satellite is repeatedly visited. Incorporate the docking capability into risk models for satellite constellations, especially those relying on low‑Earth‑orbit assets. Policy makers should consider confidence‑building measures, such as sharing orbital data, while maintaining vigilance over dual‑use technologies.
