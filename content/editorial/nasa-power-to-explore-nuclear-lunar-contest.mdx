On November 17, NASA launched the Power to Explore Student Challenge, a nationwide contest for kindergarten through 12th‑grade students. Participants must submit a short essay and an original illustration that envision how nuclear power could sustain a lunar base. The competition aims to spark early interest in space nuclear engineering as NASA prepares for a permanent presence on the Moon. By engaging young minds, the agency hopes to build a pipeline of future scientists and engineers who understand the unique advantages of radioisotope and fission power in harsh extraterrestrial environments. The deadline for entries is March 1, 2027.

<Callout type="takeaway">Nuclear power could be the key to a sustainable human presence on the Moon, and NASA is using a student contest to inspire the next generation.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s Power to Explore Student Challenge opened on Tuesday, Nov. 17, inviting U.S. students from kindergarten to 12th grade to learn about space nuclear power systems. Entries consist of a 200‑word essay describing a personal “superpower” that would aid a lunar mission, plus an original illustration of a nuclear‑powered lunar base that incorporates radioisotope or fission solutions. Submissions are judged in three grade brackets—K‑4, 5‑8, and 9‑12—with a grand‑prize trip for two to NASA’s Glenn Research Center awarded to one winner in each bracket.

The contest reflects NASA’s broader strategy to use nuclear energy for lunar exploration, especially in permanently shadowed regions near the South Pole where sunlight is scarce. As program manager Carl Sandifer explained, nuclear power can keep habitats warm through the two‑week lunar night, power robust science payloads, and enable long‑distance rover operations.

## Why it matters
Understanding nuclear power’s role in space is essential for the Artemis program’s goal of a sustainable lunar outpost. Early exposure helps students grasp complex trade‑offs—such as safety, mass, and reliability—before they enter higher education or the workforce. Moreover, public acceptance of space nuclear systems hinges on informed citizens who can weigh benefits against perceived risks.

## How to think about it
Educators can frame the contest as a problem‑solving exercise: start with the constraints of the lunar south‑pole (no sunlight, extreme cold, long nights) and ask students to match those constraints with the attributes of radioisotope thermoelectric generators and small fission reactors. Encourage them to research real NASA missions—such as the MMRTG on Perseverance—to ground their designs in existing technology. Finally, guide students to reflect on the societal implications of deploying nuclear systems beyond Earth, fostering a balanced view of innovation and responsibility.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume nuclear power is risk‑free for lunar habitats; safety analysis and public communication remain critical.</Callout>
