On November 10, the Senate Commerce Committee convened a hearing to consider two of NASA’s most critical leadership posts: chief financial officer and inspector general. The nominees, accountant Joshua Shoemaker for CFO and longtime NASA inspector Robert Steinau, appeared before senators for a brief, largely unchallenged session. Their confirmations are among the four Senate‑required appointments that shape how the agency manages its budget and safeguards its missions. With NASA’s Artemis program and the transition to commercial space stations accelerating, the oversight roles these officials will fill could influence the agency’s fiscal discipline and security posture. The hearing therefore offers a window into the legislative priorities that will steer NASA’s next decade.

## What happened
The Senate Commerce Committee held a hearing on November 10 to consider nominations for NASA’s chief financial officer and inspector general. Joshua Shoemaker, an accountant with more than 20 years in the private sector and Mississippi state government, has served since August as a senior adviser in NASA’s Mission Support Directorate and previously was controller for the Republican National Committee. Robert Steinau, who has been with NASA’s Office of Inspector General since 2009 and acted as senior official since December 2024, previously worked for the Department of Energy’s OIG and the U.S. Secret Service.

The hearing lasted a little more than an hour and featured few questions from senators, who largely turned their attention to another nomination. Shoemaker said he would focus on financial accountability, greater visibility into program performance, transparency with Congress, and modernization of NASA’s financial systems. Steinau outlined three critical challenges for his office: returning humans to the moon, transitioning from the International Space Station to commercial stations, and strengthening cybersecurity.

## Why it matters
Effective financial oversight is essential as NASA pursues ambitious goals like Artemis lunar landings and the development of commercial low‑Earth‑orbit stations. A CFO who prioritizes transparent budgeting can help prevent cost overruns that have plagued past programs, while an inspector general focused on cybersecurity and mission integrity can safeguard critical assets against emerging threats. Congressional confidence in NASA’s fiscal stewardship also influences future appropriations, making these appointments pivotal for the agency’s long‑term viability.
