NASA has opened a public portal that lets anyone embed their name on a microchip destined for the Dragonfly rotorcraft, the agency’s upcoming mission to Saturn’s moon Titan. Submissions are accepted through Jan 15 2027, after which the names will be laser‑etched onto a chip during the spacecraft’s final assembly. Dragonfly, a nuclear‑powered, SUV‑sized rotorcraft, is slated for launch in 2028 and will spend roughly 3.3 Earth years hopping across Titan’s surface after its 2034 arrival. The initiative turns a distant scientific venture into a personal touchpoint for millions of space‑enthusiasts.

<Callout type="takeaway">Your name could travel 1 billion miles to Titan aboard Dragonfly, linking the public directly to a landmark exploration.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s public‑engagement page went live in late October, inviting anyone to submit a name that will be recorded on a silicon chip integrated into Dragonfly during its assembly phase. All entries must be received by Jan 15 2027; after that deadline the chip is sealed inside the spacecraft’s avionics bay.

Dragonfly is an unprecedented rotorcraft: an SUV‑sized, nuclear‑powered vehicle designed to fly, hover, and hop across Titan’s methane‑rich landscape. The mission targets a 2028 launch, a 2034 arrival, and a multi‑year campaign that will explore dozens of sites up to several kilometers apart, probing the moon’s organic chemistry and habitability.

The effort builds on earlier milestones—NASA’s Huygens lander touched Titan in 2005 and the Cassini orbiter surveyed it for over a decade—while echoing the public‑participation model of Mars’s Ingenuity helicopter, which captured imaginations after its record‑breaking flights.

## Why it matters
Embedding personal names on a deep‑space probe transforms a remote scientific endeavor into a shared human story, fostering public interest and support for long‑duration missions. The data Dragonfly returns could reshape our understanding of prebiotic chemistry, informing the search for life beyond Earth. Moreover, the program demonstrates a scalable model for citizen involvement in future interplanetary projects.

## How to think about it
Treat the name‑submission as a personal souvenir rather than a contribution to mission design. If you’re motivated by curiosity, use the deadline as a reminder to stay informed about Dragonfly’s progress and related discoveries. Consider pairing your submission with a habit of following mission updates, attending public briefings, or supporting broader space‑science education initiatives.

<Callout type="warning">Submitting a name does not guarantee any scientific credit or influence over Dragonfly’s flight plan; it is purely a symbolic gesture.</Callout>
