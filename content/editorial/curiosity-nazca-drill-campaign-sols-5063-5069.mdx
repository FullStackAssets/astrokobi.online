Curiosity spent the week of sols 5063‑5069 focused on the Nazca drill hole, a bedrock area notable for its disk‑shaped crystal formations. The rover collected drill cores, delivered them to the Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument, and captured high‑resolution spectra and images of several crystal targets. Simultaneously, the team gathered environmental data, including dust‑devil surveys and horizon movies, to contextualize the geological findings. These coordinated observations aim to decode the formation history of the chip‑shaped crystals and assess their potential for preserving biosignatures. Understanding these processes informs both Mars habitability studies and future sample‑return strategies.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Nazca campaign links detailed mineralogy with broader questions of Mars’ past habitability.</Callout>

## What happened
During sol 5063 the rover completed its drill sequence at Nazza, extracting core material that was transferred to SAM for thermal and evolved‑gas analysis. The following sols featured ChemCam LIBS scans of the “Ahi Veremos” crystal form and a mosaic of Mastcam images to map the surrounding terrain. Additional LIBS depth profiles targeted the “Colca” and “Bermejo” crystal formations, while a remote micro‑imager (RMI) captured a wide‑field view of rock clusters beneath the Cordillera ridge.

On sol 5065 the team executed a nighttime MAHLI mosaic of the drill block side, using white‑light and ultraviolet LEDs to highlight subtle texture differences. A planned drive moved Curiosity away from the drill site toward its southern route, while continuing environmental monitoring, including a Phobos eclipse observation.

By sol 5069 the campaign wrapped up with a final set of ChemCam LIBS observations on a resistant layer within the Nazca block and a dark float rock, complemented by another RMI mosaic of lenses beneath the Mishe Mokwa ridge.

## Why it matters
The chip‑shaped crystals may record fluid‑rock interactions that occurred billions of years ago, offering clues about past water activity and potential habitability. SAM’s chemical analyses can reveal organic compounds or mineral assemblages that are otherwise invisible to remote sensing. By integrating spectroscopy, imaging, and environmental context, the campaign builds a multi‑dimensional picture of a micro‑environment that could have sheltered life.

These data also refine target selection for future missions, especially those planning to retrieve and return samples. Demonstrating that fine‑scale mineralogy can be linked to broader planetary processes strengthens the scientific case for more ambitious exploration.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the significance of the Nazca findings, consider three layers: (1) the mineralogical signature revealed by SAM, (2) the morphological context provided by ChemCam and MAHLI, and (3) the environmental backdrop captured by Mastcam and RMI. Cross‑referencing these datasets helps distinguish between primary formation features and secondary alteration. For readers tracking Mars habitability, treat the chip‑shaped crystals as a case study in how localized geology can inform global interpretations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the presence of chip‑shaped crystals alone indicates past life; they can also form through purely inorganic processes.</Callout>
