When you look up at Perseus on a clear night, one of its brightest points flickers like a cosmic wink. That star is Algol, long known as the “Demon Star” because its light dims and brightens with uncanny regularity. Modern astronomers have measured a precise cycle of 2 days, 20 hours and 49 minutes, during which its brightness swings by a factor of about three. The star’s variability gave ancient sky‑watchers a story about Medusa’s winking eye, and today it serves as a textbook example of eclipsing binaries in a triple‑star system. Understanding Algol connects mythology, observational technique, and stellar physics.

<Callout type="takeaway">Algol’s clock‑like dimming turned myth into a laboratory for studying eclipsing triple stars.</Callout>

## What happened
Algol is a variable star whose apparent magnitude oscillates between a bright maximum and a faint minimum every 2 days, 20 hours and 49 minutes. At its peak it shines roughly three times brighter than at its trough, matching the nearby second‑magnitude star Almach, while at minimum its glow drops to the level of Epsilon Persei.

Modern observations reveal that Algol is not a single star but a triple‑star system. Two of the components form a close binary that eclipses each other, producing the regular dimming pattern, while a third star orbits the pair at a greater distance, subtly influencing the system’s dynamics.

## Why it matters
The regularity of Algol’s light curve provides a natural laboratory for testing models of stellar evolution, mass transfer, and orbital mechanics. Its eclipsing nature helped early astronomers confirm that stars can orbit one another, reshaping our view of the Milky Way as a dynamic, interacting environment. Culturally, the star’s dramatic behavior forged a myth that survived millennia, illustrating how observable phenomena can seed enduring stories.

## How to think about it
Treat Algol as a case study that bridges observation and theory. First, note the precise period and amplitude of its light curve; then map those changes to the geometry of an eclipsing binary. Next, factor in the third star’s orbital influence to appreciate how multiple bodies interact over longer timescales. Finally, reflect on how human perception turns measurable patterns into narrative, reminding you to separate cultural meaning from physical explanation.

<Callout type="warning">Do not let the mythic “Demon Star” label imply any supernatural cause; the variability is fully explained by orbital mechanics.</Callout>
