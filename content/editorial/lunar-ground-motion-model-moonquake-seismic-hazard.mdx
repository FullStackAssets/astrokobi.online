As humanity moves toward permanent lunar bases, engineers need reliable predictions of how moonquakes will affect structures. Researchers led by Tessa Williams of Slate Geotechnical Consultants have built the first lunar‑specific ground‑motion model using Apollo‑era recordings and physics‑based simulations. The model shows that strong shaking can travel beyond 1,000 km, far farther than Earth models would allow. This insight forces a rethink of seismic hazard assessments for any future lunar construction project.

<Callout type="takeaway">Moonquake shaking can travel over 1,000 km, demanding lunar‑specific seismic design.</Callout>

## What happened
The team examined seismic data from the Apollo missions and found lunar seismicity differs dramatically from Earth’s, with unusually long‑duration, low‑amplitude events that persist at great distances. Combining these observations with physics‑based simulations, they derived attenuation relationships tailored to the Moon’s regolith and interior structure. The resulting model, published in *Seismological Research Letters*, predicts significantly higher ground motions at large distances than terrestrial models, where shaking typically fades after a few hundred kilometers.

On Earth, a distance of a few hundred kilometers usually means shaking has attenuated to negligible levels. On the Moon, however, a 200 km (124 mi) separation is considered short‑to‑moderate, and shaking remains strong even past 1,000 km (621 mi) for modest‑magnitude events. This contrast highlights why Earth‑based models underestimate lunar ground motion.

## Why it matters
Accurate seismic hazard assessments are essential for designing habitats, landing pads, and other infrastructure that must survive moonquake loading. Over‑ or under‑estimating shaking can lead to costly over‑design or, worse, structural failure in the harsh lunar environment. The new model provides engineers with a realistic basis for setting design criteria, safety factors, and construction guidelines, directly influencing the feasibility and safety of long‑term lunar settlement.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a lunar construction project, start by replacing Earth‑derived attenuation curves with the lunar‑specific relationships from the new model. Account for the longer duration of shaking by extending dynamic analysis time windows. Apply conservative safety factors where data gaps exist, and treat the model as a baseline that should be refined as additional lunar seismic observations become available.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on Earth‑derived ground‑motion attenuation curves for lunar projects; they significantly underpredict shaking at distances beyond a few hundred kilometers.</Callout>
