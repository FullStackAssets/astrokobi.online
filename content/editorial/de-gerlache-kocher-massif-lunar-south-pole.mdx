A telescope captured a striking view of the De Gerla­che‑Kocher massif on October 31 as it rose along the Moon’s visible limb. The formation towers roughly 4 km above the surrounding terrain and sits just beyond the lunar south pole. Its peaks bask in near‑continuous sunlight while adjacent crater floors remain in permanent shadow, likely preserving water ice. NASA’s APOD highlighted the feature as a candidate landing zone for upcoming Artemis crewed missions. Understanding this landscape is essential for planning the first sustainable human outpost on the Moon.

<Callout type="takeaway">The De Gerla­che‑Kocher massif could become the first solar‑powered foothold on the Moon’s south pole.</Callout>

## What happened
The image released by NASA shows the De Gerla­che‑Kocher massif silhouetted against the dark lunar horizon, a view made possible by a favorable libration that tilted the Moon’s south pole toward Earth. The ridge rises about 4 km above the surrounding plains, creating a stark contrast between illuminated peaks and the deep shadows of nearby craters.

Scientists note that the permanently shadowed crater floors in this region are prime candidates for harboring deposits of water ice, a resource that could support life‑support systems and fuel production. At the same time, the massif’s high points receive sunlight for most of the lunar day, offering a reliable source of solar power for a future base.

Because the terrain combines both water‑rich shadows and sun‑lit heights, mission planners have listed the massif as a potential landing site for future Artemis crewed missions, envisioning it as a natural hub for habitation, power, and in‑situ resource utilization.

## Why it matters
The south‑pole region is the focal point of international lunar exploration strategies, and the De Gerla­che‑Kocher massif embodies the dual advantages of resource availability and energy generation. A base built on these peaks could reduce the need to transport water and power from Earth, lowering mission costs and increasing sustainability. Moreover, establishing a foothold here would demonstrate the feasibility of long‑duration human presence on an airless world, informing future missions to Mars and beyond.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the massif as a settlement site, weigh energy reliability against terrain risk. Prioritize scouting missions that map surface roughness and quantify ice deposits before committing a crewed landing. Use a phased approach: first send robotic landers to test drilling and power systems, then expand to habitats that sit on sun‑lit ridges while drawing water from adjacent shadows. Integrate this site into a broader lunar infrastructure plan that includes transport corridors to other polar resources.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume water ice is readily extractable; its distribution is patchy and extraction technology is still experimental.</Callout>
