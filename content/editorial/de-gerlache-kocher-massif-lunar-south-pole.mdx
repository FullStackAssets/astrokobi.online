On November 7, 2026 NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day featured a striking telescopic view of the De Gerla­che‑Kocher massif on the Moon’s southern limb. The image, taken on October 31 during a favorable libration, reveals a rugged formation that towers roughly 4 km above the surrounding terrain. Its peaks bask in near‑continuous sunlight while nearby crater floors stay in permanent shadow, likely preserving water ice. These contrasting conditions make the massif a focal point for Artemis planners eyeing the first sustainable human outpost on the lunar south pole.

<Callout type="takeaway">The De Gerla­che‑Kocher massif combines abundant solar energy and potential ice resources, positioning it as a prime candidate for early lunar habitation.</Callout>

## What happened
The De Gerla­che‑Kocher massif looms on the visible horizon at the Moon’s south‑pole limb, rising about 4 kilometers above its surroundings. Its rugged peaks are illuminated for most of the lunar day, while the deep crater floors at its base remain in permanent shadow, a condition that likely traps water ice deposits.

NASA’s description notes that the permanently shadowed regions could hold valuable volatiles, and the sun‑lit summits offer a reliable power source for solar arrays. Because of this dual resource environment, mission designers have listed the massif as a potential landing site for future crewed Artemis missions.

A favorable libration—a slight wobble of the Moon—enhanced the oblique view captured on October 31, allowing telescopes on Earth to image the limb with unprecedented clarity.

## Why it matters
The combination of near‑continuous sunlight and possible ice makes the south‑pole region uniquely suited for a self‑sustaining lunar base. Solar power reduces the need for nuclear generators, while in‑situ water can support life support, radiation shielding, and fuel production. Securing a foothold here could accelerate the transition from short‑term visits to a permanent presence.

Moreover, establishing infrastructure near the south pole creates a logistical hub for future exploration of permanently shadowed craters, which are among the most scientifically valuable sites for understanding lunar volatiles and the history of the inner solar system.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the massif as a settlement site, weigh three core factors: energy, resources, and accessibility. First, map solar illumination to ensure that solar arrays can meet power demands without excessive storage. Second, use orbital radar and neutron spectrometer data to confirm the presence and concentration of water ice in the shadowed craters. Third, conduct high‑resolution terrain modeling to identify safe landing zones and viable rover paths. By iterating through these layers, planners can prioritize sites that maximize resource use while minimizing risk.

<Callout type="warning">Current ice estimates rely on remote sensing; until a lander confirms deposits, any plan that assumes abundant water must include contingency supplies.</Callout>
