On October 31, a telescopic image captured the De Gerlache‑Kocher massif perched on the Moon’s southern horizon. The formation rises roughly 4 km above the surrounding terrain and sits near the lunar south pole, where permanently shadowed craters may hold water ice. NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day highlighted the massif’s near‑continuous sunlight on its peaks, a trait prized for solar power. Astronomers note that the region is under active study as a candidate landing zone for future Artemis crew missions. Its unique combination of illumination and resources makes it a focal point for lunar settlement planning.

<Callout type="takeaway">The De Gerlache‑Kocher massif could become the first lunar south‑pole base thanks to its height, sunlight, and nearby ice.</Callout>

## What happened
The APOD image released on November 7 shows the De Gerlache‑Kocher massif at the lunar limb, a view made possible by a favorable libration that tilted the Moon toward Earth. The photograph, taken with a high‑resolution telescope on October 31, reveals the rugged peaks silhouetted against the dark sky.

The massif rises about 4 km above its surroundings and straddles the boundary between permanently shadowed craters and sun‑lit slopes. Scientists point out that the shadowed basins likely contain deposits of water ice, while the peaks receive almost continuous sunlight, ideal for solar arrays.

## Why it matters
The combination of near‑continuous illumination and nearby ice makes the region a prime candidate for the next phase of Artemis, where sustainable power and in‑situ resource utilization are critical. Establishing a base here could reduce the need to ferry water and fuel from Earth, lowering mission costs and enabling longer stays. Moreover, the elevated terrain offers a clear line of sight to Earth, improving communications for both crewed and robotic operations.

## How to think about it
When evaluating lunar landing sites, weigh three pillars: illumination, resources, and terrain safety. First, map sunlight hours to ensure solar power can be generated with minimal storage. Second, identify nearby permanently shadowed regions that may hold extractable ice. Third, run high‑resolution terrain analyses to spot hazards such as steep slopes or large boulders. Balancing these factors helps prioritize sites that support both short‑term missions and long‑term settlement.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the site is ready for immediate landing; extensive scouting and hazard mitigation are still required.</Callout>
