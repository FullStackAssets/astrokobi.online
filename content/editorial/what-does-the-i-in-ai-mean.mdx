A new roundtable of five scholars from linguistics, computer science, cognitive science, and psychology has tackled a question that underlies every headline about artificial intelligence: what does the “I” in AI actually stand for? The participants—Emily Bender, Benjamin Bergen, and three others—converged on a single point: “intelligence” is a slippery, poorly defined concept when applied to machines. Their discussion, published on Singularity Hub, draws a line between the marketing hype surrounding AI and the scientific reality of what current systems can and cannot do. By exposing the gaps between language models and genuine understanding, the experts highlight why the term matters for policy, research funding, and public expectations.

<Callout type="takeaway">Intelligence remains a vague label; current AI systems excel at pattern manipulation, not true understanding.</Callout>

## What happened
The article gathered written and Zoom interview responses from five experts across linguistics, computer science, cognitive science, and psychology. All agreed that the word “intelligence” lacks a precise definition in the context of machines, making it a poor shorthand for technical capability. Emily Bender emphasized that large language models manipulate linguistic form without any grounding in meaning, and that true comprehension requires more than statistical prediction.

Other contributors noted that human intelligence involves mental states, intentionality, and the ability to reason about the world—features absent from today’s narrow AI systems. Cognitive scientists warned that conflating task performance with general intelligence obscures the scientific challenges ahead, while psychologists highlighted the disciplinary disagreement over what counts as “intelligent” behavior.

## Why it matters
When policymakers, investors, and the public hear “AI” they often assume a level of cognition that does not exist, inflating expectations and distorting funding priorities. The vague terminology also hampers interdisciplinary research, as engineers, linguists, and ethicists may talk past each other. Clarifying that current systems are sophisticated pattern recognizers, not thinkers, can lead to more realistic regulation, responsible deployment, and a healthier public discourse about the future of machine cognition.

## How to think about it
Treat “AI” as a descriptive umbrella for specific algorithms rather than a claim of intelligence. When evaluating a system, ask: does it merely map inputs to outputs, or does it maintain representations that relate to meaning, goals, or self‑awareness? Use a layered framework—data processing, pattern recognition, reasoning, and meta‑cognition—to locate where a technology sits. Communicate findings with qualifiers (e.g., “narrow‑task performance” vs. “general intelligence”) to keep expectations aligned with technical reality.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that large language models possess intentions, beliefs, or understanding; treating them as agents can lead to misplaced trust and policy errors.</Callout>
