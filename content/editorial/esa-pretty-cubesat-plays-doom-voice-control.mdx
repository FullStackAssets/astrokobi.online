On July 27, 2026, a rooftop in Oslo shouted “PRETTY play Doom Doom Doom” to an ESA‑operated CubeSat, and the satellite launched the classic 1993 shooter in orbit. The experiment, carried out by the OPS‑SAT Space Lab and amateur radio volunteers, marked the world’s first demonstration of voice‑controlled satellite operation. By proving that a spacecraft can receive, transcribe, and act on spoken commands, the test opens a path to hands‑free emergency signaling and AI‑driven mission control. The feat also showed that low‑cost hobbyist efforts can push the boundaries of space technology.

<Callout type="takeaway">Voice‑controlled satellites could let anyone in distress trigger rescue actions with a simple spoken word.</Callout>

## What happened
Radio amateurs made six failed attempts to transmit a voice command to the PRETTY CubeSat between April and July. On the seventh pass over Oslo, the transmission “PRETTY play Doom Doom Doom” was received, transcribed on board, and triggered the Doom engine, which logged a short play session. The team narrowed the satellite’s receiver bandwidth and added a voice‑recognition module supplied by Tanagra Space, enabling the system to distinguish spoken words from noise.

## Why it matters
Hands‑free voice control removes the need for complex ground‑station command sequences, potentially allowing people in remote or disaster zones to issue a simple “mayday” that a satellite can understand and relay to rescue services. Coupled with on‑board AI transcription, the technology could automate emergency alerts, environmental monitoring, and even routine satellite tasks. However, exposing a spacecraft to voice commands also creates a new attack surface that must be secured.

## How to think about it
When designing future satellite services, treat voice control as an optional interface layered on top of traditional telemetry. Start with a secure authentication token embedded in the spoken phrase, and limit voice‑activated functions to non‑critical actions such as status requests or distress alerts. Test the system under realistic noise conditions and incorporate fallback commands that require multiple confirmations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on voice commands as the sole means of executing safety‑critical maneuvers until robust authentication and error‑handling are proven.</Callout>
