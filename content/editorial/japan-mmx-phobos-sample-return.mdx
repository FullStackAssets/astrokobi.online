On October 19, Japan’s JAXA will launch the Martian Moons Exploration (MMX) spacecraft aboard an H3 rocket from Tanegashima. The mission will spend the next five years orbiting Phobos, deploying a lander, and gathering soil and pebble samples from the moon’s surface. If successful, MMX will be the first mission to return material from a Martian moon to Earth. The samples could finally settle the debate over how Phobos and Deimos formed and shed light on Mars’s early environment.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX aims to be the first to bring back Phobos material, potentially unlocking the origin of Mars’s moons.</Callout>

## What happened
The MMX spacecraft is scheduled to lift off on Monday, October 19 at 20:41 BST from the Tanegashima Space Center on an H3 launch vehicle. After a cruise to Mars, the probe will enter orbit around Phobos, the larger of the two Martian moons, and spend several months mapping the terrain.

During its five‑year cruise, the spacecraft will release a small lander that will touch down on Phobos’s surface, where it will use novel low‑gravity sampling tools to collect soil and small pebbles. Phobos’s gravity is only about 0.1 % of Earth’s, requiring specially designed drills and a gentle collection mechanism.

The mission is led by JAXA in partnership with the United States, France, Germany, and the European Space Agency, building on the agency’s proven sample‑return heritage from the Hayabusa and Hayabusa‑2 asteroid missions.

## Why it matters
Returning material from a Martian moon offers a rare window into the early solar system and the processes that shaped Mars’s environment. By analyzing the chemical and isotopic composition of Phobos, scientists hope to discriminate between competing theories—whether the moons are captured asteroids or the debris of a giant impact on Mars. The findings could also inform future crewed missions to Mars by improving our understanding of the planet’s dust and radiation environment.

## How to think about it
Treat MMX as a case study in how incremental advances—like mastering low‑gravity drilling—can enable entirely new scientific frontiers. Follow mission updates to see how engineering solutions evolve, and consider the broader implication: if Phobos samples reveal a captured‑asteroid origin, it strengthens the idea that planetary systems frequently exchange small bodies, which has ramifications for asteroid‑impact risk assessments on Earth. Conversely, an impact‑origin result would highlight the role of giant collisions in shaping planetary satellites, a factor to weigh when modeling exoplanetary systems.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that Phobos samples will be available for analysis immediately after launch; the return capsule is not scheduled to arrive on Earth until the mid‑2030s.</Callout>
