Images from NASA’s HiRISE camera have revealed that ice deposits inside Martian polar craters are not static. Over the span of nearly two decades, researchers tracked bright patches on 36 craters and saw them wax and wane with the seasons. The pattern shows that most craters gain frost in winter and lose it in summer, a behavior that mirrors Earth’s polar processes. Understanding this cycle is key to piecing together Mars’s climate history and assessing water availability for future missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Seasonal frost causes most Martian polar craters to repeatedly expand and retreat their ice deposits.</Callout>

## What happened
The international team examined HiRISE images taken between 2006 and 2025, covering Mars Years 28 through 38, to monitor bright deposits on the floors of 36 craters within the northern polar ice cap. By comparing images taken in different seasons, they could infer whether the deposits grew or shrank over time.

Out of the 36 craters, 32 displayed clear changes in brightness that correspond to seasonal ice expansion in winter and sublimation in summer. The remaining four showed no detectable variation, suggesting either a stable ice layer or surface conditions that mask seasonal effects. The researchers attribute the observed brightening to frost accumulating on crater rims and floors, which then recedes as temperatures rise.

These findings confirm that Martian polar craters experience a dynamic frost cycle, similar in concept to Earth’s seasonal snow cover, but driven by the planet’s thin CO₂‑rich atmosphere and extreme temperature swings.

## Why it matters
The seasonal behavior of crater ice provides a high‑resolution proxy for the broader water cycle on Mars. By linking local frost changes to global climate models, scientists can refine estimates of past atmospheric pressure and temperature trends. This knowledge improves predictions of where subsurface ice may be stable—critical for future lander and crewed mission site selection. Moreover, the study highlights the importance of small‑scale features in reconstructing planetary climate histories.

## How to think about it
When evaluating Martian surface images, always consider the season in which the data were collected; a bright patch in summer likely indicates residual frost, while the same area in winter may appear darker due to dust cover. Use the crater‑scale frost cycle as a baseline for testing global climate simulations—if a model cannot reproduce the observed expansion and retreat, its assumptions about atmospheric pressure or temperature may need adjustment. Finally, prioritize craters that show consistent seasonal signals when scouting for in‑situ resource utilization sites.

<Callout type="warning">Brightness variations are indirect indicators of ice; they should not be taken as precise measurements of ice thickness without corroborating radar or spectroscopic data.</Callout>
