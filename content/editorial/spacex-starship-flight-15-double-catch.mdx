SpaceX is set to lift off its Starship megarocket on Nov. 13, marking the 15th test flight of the system. The launch window opens at 5:45 p.m. ET from Starbase in Texas, and the company will attempt an unprecedented “double‑catch” of both the Super Heavy booster and the upper‑stage ship on the launch tower. If successful, the two stages would return to the same pad, dramatically shortening inspection and refurbishment cycles. The outcome could reshape how quickly orbital rockets are turned around for the next mission.

<Callout type="takeaway">A successful tower catch of both stages would prove the world’s first fully reusable orbital launch system.</Callout>

## What happened
Starship’s Flight 15 is slated for a 75‑minute window beginning at 5:45 p.m. ET, launching from SpaceX’s Starbase facility in South Texas. The mission plan calls for both stages to be recovered directly to the launch mount using the tower’s “chopstick” arms, a maneuver never attempted before.

Super Heavy is expected to separate and touch down on the pad just 6.5 minutes after liftoff. About 35 minutes after launch, the upper stage will deploy 26 next‑generation Starlink V3 satellites, the second batch of that model. The ship will remain in low‑Earth orbit for roughly 22.5 hours before performing a controlled re‑entry and a second chopsticks catch on the following afternoon.

While SpaceX has previously captured Super Heavy on three earlier test flights, the upper stage has never been recovered to the launch mount, making today’s double‑catch a critical milestone for the company’s rapid‑reuse strategy.

## Why it matters
Recovering both stages to the same pad could cut turnaround time from weeks to days, slashing launch costs and increasing flight cadence. Faster refurbishment also reduces wear on critical components, potentially extending the service life of the vehicle. For satellite operators, the ability to launch and redeploy payloads on a near‑daily schedule could accelerate constellation growth and lower market barriers.

## How to think about it
Treat the double‑catch as a proof‑of‑concept experiment rather than a routine operation. Evaluate the outcome on three axes: hardware integrity after recovery, turnaround time saved, and impact on launch economics. If the test succeeds, consider how the same recovery model could be applied to other launch providers or future vehicle designs.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the double‑catch will succeed; the mission remains high‑risk and a failure could delay Starship’s reusability roadmap.</Callout>
