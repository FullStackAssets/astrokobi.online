Two NASA‑built smallsats, nicknamed Blue and Gold, have just completed back‑to‑back Earth flybys, using a powered maneuver to boost toward Mars. The flybys occurred on Nov. 7 and Nov. 9, passing within roughly 1,600 km and 1,050 km of Earth respectively. By firing their main engines at the closest approach, the spacecraft exploited the Oberth effect, marking NASA’s first use of a powered Earth flyby for a Mars mission. The maneuvers place both probes on trajectories that will deliver them to Mars in September 2027. This approach could reshape how future missions reach the Red Planet.
<Callout type="takeaway">Powered Earth flybys let smallsats reach Mars without waiting for traditional launch windows.</Callout>
## What happened
The two ESCAPADE spacecraft, built by Rocket Lab, performed Earth flybys at altitudes of about 1,600 km (Blue) and 1,050 km (Gold). At each closest approach they ignited their main engines, using the Oberth effect to gain the extra velocity needed for the interplanetary leg.
Launched last November on Blue Origin’s New Glenn, the pair spent nearly a year loitering near the Earth‑Sun L2 point before the final gravity‑assist maneuver. Advanced Space designed the trajectory to decouple Mars missions from the two‑year launch‑window cycle, allowing launches at almost any time.
The spacecraft are now on a cruise to Mars, slated to arrive two days apart in September 2027. Each will execute an 11‑minute engine burn to enter Mars orbit and begin its science campaign.
## Why it matters
By leveraging a powered Earth flyby, NASA demonstrates a flexible, cost‑effective pathway to the Red Planet that could increase launch cadence and reduce reliance on narrow planetary alignment windows. The technique also showcases how small, commercial‑built spacecraft can perform complex deep‑space maneuvers traditionally reserved for larger missions. If successful, the method may become a template for future exploration, resource prospecting, or even crewed missions that need rapid transit options.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a Mars mission concept, consider the trade‑off between launch flexibility and the precision demands of a powered flyby. Ask whether the added complexity of a deep‑space burn is offset by the ability to launch on a more convenient schedule or with a cheaper vehicle. Map the risk profile of the engine burn against the potential cost savings and timeline benefits.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume a powered flyby eliminates all timing constraints; the engine burn must be executed within seconds of the planned peri‑Earth point to achieve the intended trajectory.</Callout>
