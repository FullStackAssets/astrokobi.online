On November 9, 2026 NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day featured a striking view of the Witch Head Nebula lit by the blue supergiant Rigel. The nebula, catalogued as IC 2118, spans roughly 50 light‑years and appears as a ghostly blue head against the Orion backdrop. Its shape, reminiscent of a Halloween witch, is carved by the intense radiation and stellar wind from Rigel. The image offers a vivid illustration of how massive stars sculpt surrounding interstellar clouds, a process that shapes future star formation.
<Callout type="takeaway">Rigel’s radiation both illuminates and sculpts the Witch Head Nebula, turning dust into a blue, witch‑shaped silhouette.</Callout>
## What happened
The APOD image shows Rigel positioned just below the nebula’s centre, its blue starlight flooding the surrounding gas and dust. The nebula’s blue hue arises because dust grains scatter short‑wavelength (blue) light more efficiently than red, the same Rayleigh scattering that makes Earth’s sky blue.

IC 2118 is a diffuse cloud of gas and dust roughly 50 light‑years across, located about 400 light‑years from Earth in the constellation Orion. Over time, Rigel’s powerful ultraviolet radiation and stellar wind have eroded the cloud’s edge, giving it the distinctive “witch‑head” silhouette.
## Why it matters
Understanding how massive stars like Rigel interact with nearby interstellar material helps astronomers trace the life cycle of matter in the galaxy, from cloud to star and back again. The visual impact of APOD also engages the public, turning complex astrophysical processes into accessible imagery that can inspire future scientists.

## How to think about it
When you encounter a nebular image, first note the illuminating source, then consider how its radiation can both light and erode the cloud; the resulting color often hints at the size of scattering particles. Use this framework to differentiate between reflection nebulae (blue‑scattered light) and emission nebulae (glowing gas), and to appreciate the role of massive stars in recycling interstellar material.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the blue hue directly measures dust temperature; the color is dominated by scattering, not thermal emission.</Callout>
