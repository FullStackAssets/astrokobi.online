The European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope has delivered its first massive data dump, and astronomers have already sifted through it to find a record haul of cosmic magnifying glasses. The analysis uncovered roughly 15,000 new strong‑gravitational‑lens candidates, pushing the known inventory from under a thousand to well over ten thousand. These lenses act as natural telescopes that amplify distant galaxies, offering a sharper view of the invisible scaffolding of dark matter. Because Euclid maps more than a third of the sky in three dimensions, the surge in lenses promises to tighten constraints on dark energy and dark matter across the observable universe.

<Callout type="takeaway">Euclid’s flood of 15,000 strong‑lens candidates dramatically expands the toolkit for probing dark matter’s distribution.</Callout>

## What happened
Launched in 2023 with a budget exceeding $1 billion, Euclid set out to map more than one‑third of the sky in three dimensions. Its first public release contains about 1.7 million gigabytes of imaging and spectroscopy, cataloguing roughly 900 million galaxies and 60 million quasars across nearly 5 % of the celestial sphere after 13 months of observation.

A global consortium of over 2,000 scientists, including more than 30 Canadians, applied machine‑learning pipelines to this dataset and identified roughly 15,000 new strong‑lensing systems. Prior to Euclid, fewer than 1,000 such lenses had been confirmed, making the odds of finding one about one in ten thousand.

## Why it matters
Strong lenses provide a unique probe of the mass distribution that bends light, allowing researchers to map dark matter in and around galaxies with unprecedented precision. With an order‑of‑magnitude increase in known lenses, statistical studies of dark matter halos become far more robust, reducing uncertainties in cosmological parameters such as the dark energy equation of state.

The expanded lens catalogue also opens a window onto the early universe: magnified background galaxies become observable, letting astronomers study star formation and galaxy assembly at epochs otherwise too faint to detect. This benefits not only cosmologists but also the broader astrophysics community seeking to understand galaxy evolution.

## How to think about it
1. Prioritize candidates with high lensing probability scores for follow‑up spectroscopy. 2. Use lens‑modeling software to reconstruct source properties and infer the mass profile of the foreground lens. 3. Cross‑match Euclid lenses with other surveys (e.g., JWST, Rubin) to combine multi‑wavelength data, improving constraints on both dark matter and galaxy evolution. 4. Incorporate realistic selection functions into statistical analyses to mitigate bias.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat all 15,000 candidates as confirmed lenses without spectroscopic verification.</Callout>
