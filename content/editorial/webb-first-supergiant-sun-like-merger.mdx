On the edge of the Andromeda galaxy, astronomers witnessed a sun‑like star plunge into a bloated supergiant, creating a merger never seen in real time. The event, nicknamed Bao, erupted in 2025 after a subtle brightening that began in 2023. NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope captured the system just 47 days after the eruption’s peak, providing the earliest follow‑up of such a stellar collision. This observation bridges the gap between theoretical models and actual data, reshaping our view of how massive stars evolve.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Webb observation delivers the first real‑time glimpse of a supergiant‑sun‑like star merger.</Callout>

## What happened
Researchers identified a binary pair that had orbited peacefully for millennia before the smaller, sun‑like companion entered the envelope of a puffy supergiant. After only seven orbits—about seven years—the two stars unified in a rapid merger, far quicker than the thousands of orbits typical for smaller binary mergers. "Stellar mergers are very, very difficult to observe in real time because they are hard to predict," said Viraj Karambelkar, the study’s first author.

James Webb observed the aftermath just 47 days after the eruption’s peak, allowing scientists to reconstruct the system’s appearance before, during, and immediately after the merger. The data revealed a sudden brightening that peaked within a week, confirming predictions that contact between a companion and a supergiant’s outer layers can produce a rapid luminosity surge.

## Why it matters
The event offers a rare empirical anchor for models of massive‑star evolution, especially the poorly understood phase when a companion star merges with an expanded primary. By capturing the merger in near real time, astronomers can refine predictions about how such interactions might trigger supernovae or influence the formation of exotic remnants like neutron stars and black holes. The findings also inform transient‑survey strategies, showing that early, high‑cadence monitoring can catch fleeting phenomena that would otherwise be missed.

## How to think about it
Treat this merger as a benchmark case rather than a universal template. Incorporate its rapid timescale into stellar‑evolution simulations, but retain flexibility for longer‑duration scenarios. When monitoring nearby galaxies, flag any gradual brightening of supergiant candidates and schedule high‑resolution follow‑up within weeks to capture possible mergers.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that all supergiant mergers will follow the same swift, week‑long brightening pattern; each system’s mass ratio and envelope structure can produce different observational signatures.</Callout>
