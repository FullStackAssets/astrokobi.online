## What happened
On Wednesday, Oct. 28, NASA’s tracker listed the Moon in the waning gibbous phase with 94 % of its surface visible. The phase follows the last quarter and precedes the next full moon, which occurs on November 24, 2026, as the lunar cycle of roughly 29.5 days repeats. With that illumination level, observers can spot Tycho, Copernicus, and Mare Imbrium unaided; binocular users can add Posidonius, Mare Humorum, and Grimaldi Basin, while telescopes reveal the Apollo 12 landing site and the Rupes Altai.

## Why it matters
The high illumination makes the Moon an excellent target for casual sky‑watchers, educational outreach, and low‑light photography, while still avoiding the glare of a full moon that can wash out faint landscape details. Knowing the exact phase helps planners schedule observations of lunar surface features, coordinate amateur telescope sessions, and align cultural events that reference the Moon’s appearance. It also serves as a reminder of the predictable 29.5‑day lunar rhythm that underpins many scientific and navigational practices.

## How to think about it
Start by locating the Moon low in the western sky after sunset; its 94 % illumination will make the bright limb stand out. Use a simple star chart or a smartphone app to pinpoint Tycho near the southern limb and Copernicus toward the eastern side. If you have binoculars, scan the lower right quadrant for Posidonius and the Mare Humorum. For deeper exploration, set up a modest telescope and aim for the Apollo 12 landing site near the western edge of Mare Imbrium. Capture the scene with a short exposure to preserve detail, and remember that the Moon will set later each night as it moves toward the next full phase.
