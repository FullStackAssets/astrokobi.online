Astronomers have long used oxygen as a cosmic yardstick, but a new Hubble‑driven survey reveals that nearby dwarf galaxies retain only a fraction of the element they forge. By examining 37 low‑mass systems with Hubble, Spitzer, the Very Large Array and the Australian Telescope Compact Array, researchers measured that just 15 % of the oxygen produced by stars remains inside these galaxies today. The missing 85 % appears to have been expelled into the circumgalactic medium, reshaping our view of how small galaxies enrich their surroundings. This discovery forces a rethink of metal‑budget calculations that underpin models of galaxy evolution and the lifecycle of the elements that make planets habitable.

<Callout type="takeaway">Dwarf galaxies keep only about 15 % of the oxygen their stars create, ejecting the rest into surrounding halos.</Callout>

## What happened
The team used Hubble imaging and spectroscopy combined with archival Spitzer infrared data and radio observations from the Very Large Array and the Australian Telescope Compact Array to map gas‑phase oxygen in 37 dwarf galaxies within roughly 10 Mpc. They quantified the oxygen produced by recent star formation and compared it to the oxygen present in the interstellar medium, stars, and dust.

The analysis shows that roughly 15 % of the produced oxygen is still detectable inside the galaxies, mostly in the gas phase, with smaller contributions from stars and dust. The remaining oxygen is inferred to reside in the galaxies’ circumgalactic medium or to have been expelled into intergalactic space.

## Why it matters
Oxygen is the third most abundant element and a key tracer of chemical enrichment; its loss from dwarf galaxies means that a substantial portion of metals is deposited outside the visible galaxy, influencing the composition of the CGM and the broader intergalactic medium. This metal outflow impacts star‑formation efficiency, the growth of larger galaxies that accrete dwarf material, and the inventory of elements available for future planetary systems.

## How to think about it
When building models of galaxy evolution, treat dwarf galaxies as net metal exporters rather than closed boxes; incorporate CGM oxygen reservoirs into mass‑balance calculations, and be cautious about using dwarf metallicities as proxies for total metal production.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the oxygen measured inside a dwarf galaxy accounts for all the metals it has produced; a large unseen reservoir likely exists in its halo.</Callout>
