As the nights grow longer and the leaves turn, a familiar summer pattern reappears in the autumn sky. The Summer Triangle—a bright asterism of Vega, Altair and Deneb—shifts westward after sunset in November. Spotting it now offers a quick way to connect with the night sky as the season changes, and it reminds us how celestial cycles persist beyond our calendars.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Summer Triangle remains visible in the western evening sky throughout November and December, offering a simple guide for autumn stargazers.</Callout>

## What happened
The Summer Triangle is not a constellation but an asterism formed by three luminous stars in separate constellations: Vega in Lyra, Altair in Aquila, and Deneb in Cygnus. Vega sits about 25 light‑years from Earth and shines with a blue‑white hue, making it the fifth‑brightest star in the night sky. Altair, roughly 16.7 light‑years away, appears flanked by two fainter companions and sets around 10 – 11 p.m. at mid‑northern latitudes in November. Deneb, the most distant of the trio at roughly 1,400‑2,600 light‑years, is a supergiant that looks fainter to the naked eye.

During the autumn months the Triangle rises in the west after sunset, staying above the horizon until mid‑to‑late evening. Each night it appears about four minutes earlier, accumulating to roughly two hours earlier each month, so observers can track its gradual drift westward.

## Why it matters
Seeing the Summer Triangle in autumn bridges the seasonal gap between the iconic summer sky and the winter constellations, reinforcing public interest in astronomy. Its bright stars serve as easy reference points for beginners learning to navigate the night sky, and they have historical roles in navigation and myth. Moreover, the asterism’s visibility highlights the impact of light pollution: in bright urban areas the faint Deneb may be lost, underscoring the need for darker sites to experience the full pattern.

## How to think about it
Start by checking the local sunset time and aim to look westward about 30 minutes after dark. Identify Vega first—its blue‑white color and prominence make it a reliable anchor. Then locate Altair, noting its position near the horizon as it will set soon. Finally, sweep eastward to find Deneb, which may require a darker site. Using a star‑chart app set to the current date will show the Triangle’s exact altitude and help you time your observation before Altair disappears.
