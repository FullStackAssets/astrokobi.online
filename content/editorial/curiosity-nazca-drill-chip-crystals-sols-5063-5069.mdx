Curiosity spent Sols 5063‑5069 focused on a deep drill campaign at the Nazca site, an area of bedrock dotted with unusual disk‑ or chip‑shaped crystal formations. The rover delivered drill cores to the Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument while simultaneously capturing ChemCam LIBS spectra of nearby targets such as “Ahi Veremos,” “Colca,” and “Bermejo.” Complementary imaging—including Mastcam mosaics, MAHLI nighttime LED shots, and long‑distance RMI mosaics—provided a multi‑scale context for the mineralogy. These coordinated observations aim to decipher whether the chip‑shaped features are sedimentary, volcanic, or the product of post‑depositional alteration, a question central to Mars’ habitability narrative.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Nazca drill reveals unprecedented chip‑shaped crystal formations that could reshape our understanding of Martian sedimentary processes.</Callout>

## What happened
The week began with Curiosity completing its drill at the Nazca hole, extracting material from bedrock that hosts a suite of flat, disk‑like crystals. SAM began on‑board analysis of the powdered sample, targeting volatile compounds and isotopic ratios to infer formation conditions. On Monday, ChemCam performed LIBS observations of the newly identified “Ahi Veremos” crystal form while Mastcam added frames to a growing mosaic of the Nazca field.

Wednesday’s plan added two targeted science blocks: a depth‑profile LIBS scan of the “Colca” crystals and a separate LIBS measurement of the “Bermejo” target. A remote micro‑imager (RMI) captured a long‑distance mosaic of rock clusters beneath the “Cordillera” ridge, extending the geological context beyond the immediate drill site.

Friday’s activities included a drive away from Nazca, a MAHLI mosaic of the block side containing the drill hole, and nighttime imaging of the crystal forms using white‑light and UV LEDs. The rover also recorded Phobos eclipsing the Sun, and ChemCam continued LIBS scans of a dark float rock and a resistant layer on the Nazca block. A final RMI mosaic surveyed dark lenses beneath the “Mishe Mokwa” ridge.

## Why it matters
The chip‑shaped crystals represent a morphology rarely seen in previous Martian datasets, suggesting either a unique depositional environment or a post‑depositional process such as sublimation or diagenesis. SAM’s chemical fingerprints can confirm whether these features contain hydrated minerals, a key indicator of past water activity. By coupling composition with high‑resolution imaging, scientists can refine models of sediment transport and alteration on early Mars, informing the selection of future sample‑return sites that maximize the potential for biosignature preservation.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the Nazca findings, adopt a three‑step framework: (1) characterize morphology with imaging (size, aspect ratio, surface texture); (2) cross‑reference composition from SAM and LIBS to identify hydration or volcanic signatures; (3) place the results within regional geology using RMI and Mastcam mosaics. This approach helps separate primary formation processes from secondary alteration, guiding both scientific interpretation and future mission planning.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the chip‑shaped crystals are definitive evidence of ancient water without corroborating mineralogical data from SAM.</Callout>
