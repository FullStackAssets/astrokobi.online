A team of international astronomers has announced the discovery of J1602+4228AB, the earliest known compact pair of quasars. Nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, the duo shines brightly just 900 million years after the Big Bang, making it the most distant and tightly spaced quasar pair ever observed. The pair was identified through a combination of Gemini North imaging and James Webb Space Telescope spectroscopy. Its existence forces scientists to rethink how supermassive black holes could grow so massive in the universe’s first billion years.

<Callout type="takeaway">Compact quasar pairs can form far earlier than current black‑hole growth models predict.</Callout>

## What happened
The researchers detected a close‑separation source in the Aether survey and followed up with high‑resolution Gemini North observations, confirming two active nuclei separated by only a few kiloparsecs. Spectroscopic data from JWST revealed both components to be luminous quasars at a redshift of z≈6.2, corresponding to a cosmic age of roughly 900 Myr. The system, designated J1602+4228AB, has been given the Hawaiian nickname Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, meaning “the twin flames of the early universe.”

These quasars are unusually massive for their epoch, each hosting a black hole estimated to be several hundred million solar masses. Their proximity suggests they reside in a merging galaxy pair, providing a rare glimpse of black‑hole growth during the era known as cosmic dawn.

## Why it matters
The discovery directly challenges the standard picture in which supermassive black holes grow gradually from stellar‑mass seeds via steady accretion. To reach such masses in less than a billion years, the black holes must have experienced either extremely efficient accretion or begun as unusually massive seeds. Understanding this process informs models of early galaxy formation, the reionization timeline, and the role of mergers in fueling black‑hole growth.

## How to think about it
When evaluating early‑universe black holes, start by questioning the assumed seed mass distribution and the maximum sustainable accretion rate. Compare observed luminosities with theoretical growth tracks, and consider whether rapid, merger‑driven inflows could temporarily boost accretion. Use Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua as a benchmark case: assess how its host‑galaxy environment, merger state, and gas supply might have enabled its rapid growth, then test those conditions against cosmological simulations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not extrapolate from this single object to claim that all early supermassive black holes grew via the same rapid channel.</Callout>
