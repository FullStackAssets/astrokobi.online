The universe’s first billion years have just yielded a surprise: an exceptionally close pair of quasars, J1602+4228AB, has been spotted at a time when the cosmos was only 900 million years old. Detected with Gemini North and the James Webb Space Telescope, the duo—nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua—represents the most distant and compact quasar pair ever recorded. Their existence forces astronomers to confront a gap in models of how supermassive black holes can grow so rapidly. If such massive black holes can already be active in tight binaries, the pathways of early galaxy evolution may need revision. This discovery therefore reshapes our view of black‑hole assembly during cosmic dawn.

<Callout type="takeaway">Compact quasar pairs can form within the first billion years, challenging current black‑hole growth theories.</Callout>

## What happened
An international team led by postdoctoral researcher Silvia Onorato used Gemini North on Maunakea and JWST to identify J1602+4228AB, a pair of actively feeding supermassive black holes separated by a few kiloparsecs. The system, officially designated Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, lies at a redshift that places it roughly 900 million years after the Big Bang, making it the earliest known compact quasar duo.

The pair’s compactness and brightness were confirmed through spectroscopic follow‑up, revealing both objects as luminous quasars whose emission lines match expectations for high‑redshift active galactic nuclei. Such close quasar pairs are predicted to be common in hierarchical galaxy formation, yet finding one at this epoch is exceptionally rare, highlighting the power of deep infrared surveys like the Aether program.

## Why it matters
The discovery challenges the prevailing view that supermassive black holes need several hundred million years of sustained accretion to reach quasar luminosities. A binary system this massive at cosmic dawn suggests either unusually efficient feeding mechanisms or alternative formation pathways, such as direct‑collapse black holes. Understanding these processes impacts models of early galaxy assembly, the reionization timeline, and the expected gravitational‑wave background from massive black‑hole mergers.

## How to think about it
When evaluating early‑universe black‑hole discoveries, weigh the observational constraints against theoretical expectations. Treat J1602+4228AB as a proof‑of‑concept that rapid growth pathways exist, but avoid extrapolating from one object to the whole population. Follow upcoming JWST deep fields and ground‑based spectroscopy to see whether similar pairs emerge, which will either reinforce or dilute the current puzzle.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that all high‑redshift quasars are part of binary systems; the rarity of this detection means most early quasars remain isolated in current surveys.</Callout>
