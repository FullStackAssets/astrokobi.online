NASA has set a launch window for the Northrop Grumman CRS-25 mission, targeting no earlier than 10:47 a.m. EST on Sunday, Nov 29. The Cygnus XL spacecraft, named S.S. James “Jim” A. Lovell, will ride aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral’s SLC‑40. The flight will deliver roughly 11,000 lb of scientific investigations, supplies, and equipment to the International Space Station. A pre‑launch media teleconference is scheduled for Monday, Nov 23, giving journalists a chance to hear from NASA, Northrop Grumman, and SpaceX officials. Live coverage of both launch and subsequent docking will be streamed across NASA’s digital platforms.

<Callout type="takeaway">The CRS‑25 launch will replenish the ISS while advancing biomedical and food‑production research for long‑duration missions.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA, Northrop Grumman, and SpaceX have coordinated a launch window of no earlier than 10:47 a.m. EST on Nov 29, using a Falcon 9 from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station. The Cygnus XL vehicle, designated S.S. James “Jim” A. Lovell, will carry approximately 11,000 lb of cargo to the orbiting laboratory.

The payload includes experiments aimed at improving drug delivery for cancer and osteoarthritis, predicting cancer spread, mitigating muscle loss, enhancing radiation sensing, and advancing food production for future long‑duration missions. After docking, astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney will use Canadarm2 to capture the spacecraft on Dec 1 and install it on the Unity module’s Earth‑facing port for unloading.

A pre‑launch teleconference on Nov 23 will feature NASA’s Gaurang Patel and Jennifer Buchli, Northrop Grumman’s Dave Schiller, and SpaceX’s Jared Metter, providing media with direct updates before the flight.

## Why it matters
The CRS‑25 mission sustains the ISS crew’s daily operations while delivering cutting‑edge biomedical research that could shape health protocols for future lunar and Martian habitats. By leveraging commercial launch services, NASA continues to reduce costs and increase launch cadence, reinforcing the public‑private partnership model that underpins the Commercial Resupply Services program. The scientific payload also tests technologies—such as advanced radiation sensors and closed‑loop food systems—that are critical for the next generation of deep‑space exploration.

## How to think about it
If you want to follow the mission, bookmark NASA’s live‑stream page and set a reminder for the 10:47 a.m. EST launch window on Nov 29. Consider the broader trend: each successful commercial resupply flight not only keeps the ISS operational but also de‑risky the logistics chain for Artemis and eventual Mars missions. Use this launch as a case study for how public‑sector goals—science, crew health, and exploration—are being met through private‑sector launch capability.
