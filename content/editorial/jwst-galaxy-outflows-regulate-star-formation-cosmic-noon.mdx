When the universe was at its most prolific in birthing stars, massive galaxies were already turning off the taps. New JWST data show that these galaxies launch vast streams of gas that escape into intergalactic space. By stripping away the cold fuel needed for new stars, the outflows can suppress further star formation, reshaping galaxy evolution. The study, now on arXiv, links these winds directly to the decline in star‑forming activity during the era known as cosmic noon.

<Callout type="takeaway">Powerful galactic outflows are the primary mechanism throttling star birth at the universe’s peak.</Callout>

## What happened
JWST observations of galaxies at redshifts corresponding to 8–12 billion years ago reveal large‑scale gaseous outflows extending well beyond the central regions. Spectroscopic data show high‑velocity gas moving outward, indicating that substantial amounts of cold, star‑forming material are being expelled.

These outflows are driven by two main engines: energetic bursts from supermassive black holes and the collective effect of supernova explosions. Both mechanisms inject momentum into the interstellar medium, lifting gas out of the galaxy’s gravitational well.

The measured outflows are more abundant and energetic than earlier surveys suggested, implying that feedback from these winds plays a dominant role in halting star formation during the universe’s peak epoch.

## Why it matters
If outflows routinely strip galaxies of their star‑forming fuel, theoretical models of galaxy growth must account for a stronger feedback loop than previously assumed. This reshapes our understanding of why the cosmic star‑formation rate peaked and then declined. It also informs predictions about the future evolution of massive galaxies, including our own Milky Way, as they transition from active star‑forming systems to quiescent ones.

## How to think about it
When evaluating galaxy evolution, treat outflows as a regulatory valve rather than a peripheral side effect. Incorporate measured outflow rates and energies into semi‑analytic models to test how quickly a galaxy can exhaust its cold gas reservoir. For observers, prioritize multi‑wavelength follow‑up to separate ionized, atomic, and molecular components of the wind, which together determine the total impact on star formation.

<Callout type="warning">Do not extrapolate the outflow strength measured in a few luminous galaxies to the entire population without accounting for selection bias.</Callout>
