The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft is now within 60 km of the binary asteroid Didymos, capturing its first close‑up of the system after the 2022 DART impact on Dimorphos. At the same time NASA is repurposing test‑bed rovers into the PROMISE mission slated for a 2028 launch to the lunar south pole, while Voyager 1 will cross the one‑light‑day distance from Earth on 18 Nov 2026. These events together illustrate how the outcomes of past missions are feeding new scientific and operational strategies. Understanding the legacies of these programs helps us gauge the balance between low‑cost experiments and large‑scale exploration.

<Callout type="takeaway">The combined legacies of Hera, PROMISE and Voyager 1 are reshaping how we plan and fund future space exploration.</Callout>

## What happened
Hera, traveling at a crawl of 12 cm s⁻¹, captured an image of Didymos from 60 km away, showing Dimorphos—the DART‑impacted moonlet—still orbiting its primary. The spacecraft’s slow orbit allows scientists to map the binary system’s dynamics with unprecedented precision, setting the stage for detailed impact‑effect studies.

NASA announced that the PROMISE rover will be assembled from test‑bed hardware used on Perseverance and Curiosity, combined with instruments from the VIPER lunar rover. The mission aims to explore the lunar south pole for 18 months, launching in 2028, and is positioned as a cost‑effective way to extend rover technology to new environments.

Meanwhile, Voyager 1 is set to become the first human‑made object to travel one light‑day from Earth on 18 Nov 2026, a symbolic milestone celebrated with a livestream. NASA also opened a new astronaut application round, due 3 Dec, underscoring the agency’s ongoing human‑spaceflight ambitions.

## Why it matters
Hera’s measurements will validate kinetic‑impact deflection models, directly informing planetary‑defense strategies. PROMISE demonstrates how reusing existing hardware can accelerate lunar science while keeping budgets in check, but its reliance on older components raises reliability questions. Voyager’s light‑day milestone reminds us of the longevity of deep‑space probes and the scientific value of long‑duration missions, influencing future investment decisions across agencies.

## How to think about it
When evaluating future missions, weigh the scientific payoff against the maturity of the hardware. Use Hera’s data as a benchmark for any kinetic‑impact concept, and treat PROMISE as a case study in modular, up‑gradable rover design. Consider Voyager’s longevity as a reminder that mission value can accrue far beyond the primary objectives, encouraging planners to embed long‑term data collection capabilities from the start.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that reusing test‑bed hardware automatically guarantees mission success; thorough qualification testing remains essential.</Callout>
