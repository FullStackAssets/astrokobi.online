The latest episode of This Week In Space, #236, turned its attention to the limits of how we imagine extraterrestrial life. Host Tariq Malik and Rod Pyle welcomed astrobiologist Mikhail Tikhonov to argue that life could look nothing like the carbon‑based microbes we typically search for. Tikhonov proposes a functional definition—any system that captures energy, transforms it, and replicates—that widens the search horizon. The discussion arrives as SpaceX prepares a historic Starship launch, reminding listeners that our exploration tools are evolving alongside our ideas. Understanding these broader possibilities could reshape future missions and detection strategies.
<Callout type="takeaway">Life may be any energy‑transforming, self‑replicating system, not just carbon‑based microbes.</Callout>
## What happened
In the episode, Tikhonov explained that the classic view of alien life—silicon‑based or carbon‑based organisms resembling Earth microbes—reflects a bias rooted in terrestrial biology. He suggested that the defining traits of life are the ability to take in energy, convert it, and produce copies of itself, a definition that could include plasma clouds, crystal lattices, or even engineered nanostructures.
The hosts explored how this broader definition would affect observational strategies, from spectroscopic surveys of exoplanet atmospheres to the design of future probes that could detect non‑chemical signatures. They also noted that the upcoming Starship double‑catch test demonstrates how rapidly our launch capabilities are expanding, potentially enabling more ambitious sample‑return and in‑situ investigations of exotic environments.
## Why it matters
If life can exist in forms that do not rely on carbon chemistry, the pool of worlds worth investigating expands dramatically, from icy moons to high‑temperature exoplanets previously dismissed. Mission planners may need to equip probes with sensors capable of detecting energy flows or replication patterns rather than just chemical markers. This shift also invites collaboration between physicists, materials scientists, and computer engineers to recognize signatures that current astrobiology frameworks overlook.

## How to think about it
Treat the functional definition as a checklist: does the candidate system capture external energy, transform that energy into internal work, and produce copies of itself? Apply this checklist when evaluating new data, but prioritize signals that can be independently verified with multiple detection methods. Align mission concepts with this broader view early in the design phase to avoid retrofitting instruments later.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that existing telescopic surveys are already capable of detecting these unconventional life forms; new instrumentation and analysis methods are required.</Callout>
