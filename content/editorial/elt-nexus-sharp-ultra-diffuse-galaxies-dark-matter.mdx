The Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is slated for its first light in March 2029, and one of its flagship instruments, NEXUS@SHARP, is being built to tackle one of cosmology’s toughest puzzles: the dark‑matter content of ultra‑diffuse galaxies (UDGs). UDGs are the size of the Milky Way but contain far fewer stars, making them appear ghostly and difficult to study with existing spectrographs. By targeting the globular clusters that orbit these faint systems, NEXUS@SHARP promises to map their surrounding dark halos for the first time. The effort builds on a recent New Astronomy paper that outlines how the spectrograph can resolve individual cluster velocities beyond the local group. If successful, the observations could confirm or overturn key assumptions of the ΛCDM model.

<Callout type="takeaway">The ELT will finally let us weigh the dark halos of the universe’s fluffiest galaxies.</Callout>

## What happened
The ELT’s first‑light window is set for March 2029, and the NEXUS@SHARP instrument pairs a multi‑object spectrograph (NEXUS) with the high‑angular‑resolution SHARP spectrograph. According to the New Astronomy article led by Marco Mirabile, this combination will reach the depth needed to obtain spectra of individual globular clusters around UDGs well beyond the Local Group.

Ultra‑diffuse galaxies challenge ΛCDM because they are as large as the Milky Way yet host thousands of times fewer stars, resulting in extremely low surface brightness. Their diffuse stellar distribution makes traditional spectroscopy ineffective, but globular clusters act as bright tracers of the underlying dark‑matter halo, offering a way to measure mass profiles that were previously inaccessible.

## Why it matters
If NEXUS@SHARP can reliably determine halo masses for a representative sample of UDGs, it will provide a direct test of whether these objects sit in massive dark‑matter halos as ΛCDM predicts, or whether alternative formation pathways (e.g., tidal stripping or feedback‑driven puffing) dominate. The outcome influences our understanding of galaxy formation, the nature of dark matter, and the validity of the standard cosmological model.

## How to think about it
Treat ELT UDG measurements as a new data point in a broader framework: compare dynamical masses to predictions from cosmological simulations, cross‑check with independent tracers (e.g., stellar kinematics, weak lensing), and remain aware of systematic uncertainties tied to cluster selection and orbital modeling.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat early ELT measurements as definitive proof of any dark‑matter model until the instrument’s performance is fully calibrated.</Callout>
