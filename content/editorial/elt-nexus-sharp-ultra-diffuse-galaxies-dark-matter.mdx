The European Southern Observatory’s Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is slated to see first light in March 2029, bringing a 39‑meter aperture to the night sky. Among its first‑light instruments is NEXUS@SHARP, a multi‑object spectrograph paired with the high‑resolution SHARP spectrograph. New research published in New Astronomy demonstrates how this combination can obtain spectra of globular clusters embedded in ultra‑diffuse galaxies (UDGs) far beyond the Local Group. Because globular clusters trace the mass of their host halos, the observations promise a direct test of dark‑matter predictions in systems that currently strain the Lambda‑CDM model.

<Callout type="takeaway">NEXUS@SHARP will let the ELT weigh the dark‑matter halos of the universe’s flattest galaxies.</Callout>

## What happened
The ELT, a 39‑meter ground‑based telescope, is scheduled for first light in March 2029 and will host a suite of instruments designed to tackle fundamental cosmological questions, including the nature of dark matter. One of these instruments, NEXUS@SHARP, couples a multi‑object spectrograph (NEXUS) with the high‑angular‑resolution SHARP spectrograph, enabling deep, high‑resolution spectroscopy of faint targets.

A paper in New Astronomy by Marco Mirabile and collaborators outlines how NEXUS@SHARP can target globular clusters (GCs) in ultra‑diffuse galaxies. UDGs are as large as the Milky Way but contain thousands of times fewer stars, making them extremely low‑surface‑brightness objects that challenge standard galaxy‑formation models within the ΛCDM framework. By measuring the velocities of GCs orbiting these galaxies, astronomers can infer the mass and distribution of their dark‑matter halos.

## Why it matters
If UDGs truly reside in massive dark‑matter halos, they could represent a missing link in hierarchical galaxy formation, forcing revisions to ΛCDM simulations. Conversely, if they are found to be dark‑matter deficient, alternative gravity theories may gain traction. The ability to directly weigh these halos therefore impacts our understanding of dark‑matter particle properties, the efficiency of star formation in low‑density environments, and the overall architecture of cosmic structure.

## How to think about it
Treat ELT spectroscopic results as a new, high‑precision constraint rather than a definitive verdict. Combine the measured GC velocity dispersions with cosmological simulations to explore a range of halo‑mass scenarios, and remain mindful of selection effects that favor brighter, nearer UDGs. By iterating between observations and model predictions, the community can gradually narrow the viable dark‑matter parameter space.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume ELT data will instantly solve the dark‑matter puzzle; results will be model‑dependent and limited by early‑science constraints.</Callout>
