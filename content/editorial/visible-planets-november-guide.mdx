The November sky is a showcase of planetary brilliance and a burst of meteors, giving stargazers a rich set of targets. Jupiter and Mars will share the dawn horizon on the morning of the 17th, aligning within about 1.2 degrees of each other near the Leonids’ radiant point. The Leonid meteor shower peaks on the nights of the 17th and 18th, offering a chance to see dozens of fast meteors per hour. Throughout the month the Moon will pair with Saturn, a supermoon, and bright stars like Aldebaran, Pleiades, and Regulus, creating striking silhouettes. Knowing when and where to look turns a casual glance into a rewarding observing session.

<Callout type="takeaway">November’s sky delivers a dense lineup of planets, a meteor shower, and lunar pairings that are easy to catch with a simple plan.</Callout>

## What happened
On the morning of November 17, Jupiter and Mars rise together in the east, their disks separated by roughly 1.2 degrees, placing them almost on top of the Leonids’ radiant. The Leonid meteor shower, active on the 17th and 18th, will produce bright, fast meteors that trace back to this same point.

Evening views shift as the Moon moves across the sky: on November 19‑20 it sits near Saturn, on the 23‑24 it becomes a supermoon close to Aldebaran and the Pleiades, and on November 29‑30 the Moon aligns with Jupiter, Mars and the bright star Regulus.

Additional highlights include Mercury reaching its greatest elongation from the morning Sun, and a series of bright‑star pairings on November 25‑27, such as the twin stars of Gemini and the star Capella in the northeast.

## Why it matters
These events give amateur astronomers a rare, low‑tech opportunity to practice sky‑reading skills, capture striking astrophotography, and connect cultural stories to real celestial motions. The Leonids provide a meteor‑watching spectacle that can boost public interest in space science, while the planetary conjunctions and supermoon create natural landmarks for teaching orbital dynamics. Light‑polluted observers can still benefit from the bright planets and Moon, but timing and location become critical for catching the finer details.

## How to think about it
Start by checking a location‑specific sky app (Stellarium, SkySafari, or a mobile planetarium) for rise and set times at your latitude. Prioritize dark‑sky sites for the Leonids, and plan short observing windows for the Jupiter‑Mars pairing and Moon‑planet alignments. Bring a simple red‑light flashlight to preserve night vision, and use a wide‑field binoculars or a DSLR with a short focal length to capture the supermoon and surrounding constellations. Record the dates and times you observe; the pattern repeats annually, helping you build a personal sky‑watching calendar.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on generic sky charts for exact rise times; use a location‑specific tool like Stellarium or a mobile app for precise timing.</Callout>
