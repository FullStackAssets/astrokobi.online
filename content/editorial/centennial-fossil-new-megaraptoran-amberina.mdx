Hidden in a dusty drawer at the Natural History Museum for nearly a century, a partial dinosaur skeleton from the Isle of Wight has finally been examined with modern technology. Researchers from University College London used high‑resolution CT scanning and 3D modeling to reveal that the specimen represents a previously unknown megaraptoran, which they named Invictibellatrix amberina. The find reshapes our picture of mid‑Cretaceous predators in Europe and highlights the untapped scientific value of museum backlogs.

<Callout type="takeaway">A century‑old fossil can still rewrite dinosaur evolution when modern imaging is applied.</Callout>

## What happened
The specimen was originally collected by amateur paleontologist Reginald Walter Tooley in the early 1900s and entered the Natural History Museum’s storage as part of a batch of roughly 1,000 unstudied fossils. It remained untouched in a drawer for almost 100 years, unnoticed by researchers.

In 2024, paleontologists Cassius Morrison and his student Charlie Scherer at University College London retrieved the fossil and subjected it to high‑resolution computed‑tomography. The scans revealed a distinctive hour‑glass‑shaped cranial crest and an oval foramen that set the animal apart from known megaraptorans. After 3D reconstruction, they published the description in Royal Society Open Science on October 14, 2026, formally naming the species Invictibellatrix amberina.

## Why it matters
The discovery proves that museum collections still hold undiscovered diversity, especially for groups like megaraptorans that are poorly represented in Europe. It also demonstrates how non‑destructive imaging can extract anatomical detail from fragile specimens, expanding the toolkit for paleobiology and informing models of Cretaceous ecosystem dynamics.

## How to think about it
When encountering a legacy specimen, start by assessing its preservation and uniqueness through visual inspection, then prioritize it for high‑resolution imaging if it shows atypical features. Pair the scan data with comparative anatomy and phylogenetic analysis to test whether it represents a new taxon. Finally, document the workflow and share raw scan files to enable reproducibility and collaborative verification.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that every archived fossil will produce a novel species; careful triage and adequate funding are essential before committing high‑cost imaging resources.</Callout>
