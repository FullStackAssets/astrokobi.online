The latest episode of This Week In Space (Episode 236) turned a spotlight on how we define life, featuring physicist Mikhail Tikhonov’s provocative ideas. Instead of the familiar carbon‑based microbes, Tikhonov argues that any system that captures energy, transforms it, and replicates qualifies as life. This broader definition could reshape the way scientists design experiments to detect extraterrestrials. Listeners heard the hosts Tariq Malik and Rod Pyle explore the implications for future missions and for the public imagination.

<Callout type="takeaway">A broader, energy‑based definition of life expands the search for alien organisms beyond carbon‑based expectations.</Callout>

## What happened
Tariq Malik and Rod Pyle welcomed Washington University researcher Mikhail Tikhonov to discuss how traditional assumptions about extraterrestrial life may be too narrow. Tikhonov proposed a new definition that focuses on energy capture, transformation, and replication, rather than specific chemistry.

He cited examples ranging from hypothetical silicon‑based metabolisms to self‑replicating plasma structures, emphasizing that life could exist in environments previously dismissed as sterile. The conversation highlighted how current biosignature missions are designed around carbon‑based markers and how a shift in definition could open new observational strategies.

## Why it matters
Expanding the definition of life changes the target set for astrobiology missions, potentially guiding telescopes to look for energy flux patterns instead of only chemical fingerprints. This could accelerate the discovery of life on worlds like Venus or icy moons where traditional biosignatures are ambiguous. It also challenges the scientific community to confront anthropocentric bias, influencing funding priorities and public expectations about finding aliens.

## How to think about it
Adopt a flexible detection framework that prioritizes measurable energy flows, such as anomalous heat or radiation patterns, alongside traditional chemical analyses. Researchers should model a broader suite of theoretical life chemistries and test instrument sensitivity against those scenarios. Policy makers can allocate exploratory funding to high‑risk, high‑reward projects that investigate non‑carbon signatures, while maintaining rigorous validation protocols.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely solely on Earth‑centric chemical signatures when designing life‑detection experiments.</Callout>
