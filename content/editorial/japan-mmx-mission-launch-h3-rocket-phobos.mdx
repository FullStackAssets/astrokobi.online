On Tuesday at 4:41 a.m. GMT, Japan’s JAXA will ignite the H3 rocket from Tanegashima to send the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) probe toward the Red Planet’s moon Phobos. The mission is designed to orbit Phobos, touch down, and retrieve at least 10 grams of surface material before heading back to Earth. A French‑named rover, IDEFIX, will scout the landing site and conduct observations of both Phobos and the distant moon Deimos. If successful, MMX will become the first spacecraft to complete a round‑trip to a Martian moon, opening a new chapter in planetary science and human‑Mars exploration.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX will be the first round‑trip mission to a Martian moon, delivering the inaugural samples from Phobos.</Callout>

## What happened
The H3 launch vehicle, Japan’s newest heavy‑lift rocket, lifted off from the Tanegashima Space Center at 4:41 a.m. GMT, carrying the MMX probe built in partnership with U.S. and European agencies and several universities. The spacecraft will cruise to Mars over the next year, aiming to reach the Martian system in August 2027, where it will enter orbit around Phobos.

Once in orbit, MMX will descend to Phobos’s surface, where the IDEFIX rover will perform a pre‑landing survey to identify a safe touchdown zone. The lander will then collect a minimum of 10 grams of regolith, store it in a sealed container, and later lift off to rendezvous with the orbiter for the return journey to Earth. During its three‑year stay, the probe will also fly past Deimos to acquire remote‑sensing data before departing the Martian system.

Scientists hope the returned material will clarify how Phobos and Deimos formed—testing the “giant impact” hypothesis against the “capture” scenario—and provide clues about the early solar system and the conditions that led to life on Earth.

## Why it matters
By delivering the first physical samples from a Martian moon, MMX could resolve long‑standing debates about the moons’ origins, informing models of planetary formation across the solar system. The mission also evaluates Phobos as a potential “natural space station,” a stepping stone that could reduce the energy cost of crewed Mars missions. Moreover, the international collaboration showcases a template for future deep‑space endeavors, spreading risk and expertise among partner nations.

## How to think about it
Treat MMX as a proof‑of‑concept for a phased Mars architecture: first, demonstrate autonomous sample return from a small body; second, use the data to design a Phobos‑based fuel depot or habitat; third, integrate those capabilities into a crewed Mars transit plan. For readers, follow the mission’s data releases, consider how the sample analysis could reshape models of planetary accretion, and watch how the international partnership evolves for future deep‑space projects.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the 10‑gram sample will instantly answer the moon‑origin question; detailed laboratory work and comparative analysis will be required over many years.</Callout>
