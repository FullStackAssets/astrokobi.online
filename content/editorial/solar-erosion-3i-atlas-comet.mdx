A handful of interstellar visitors have let us peek at material forged around other stars. As 3I/ATLAS swooped through the inner solar system, the Sun’s intense heat appears to have stripped a measurable skin from its icy nucleus. By combining published gas‑escape measurements with a simple spherical model, researchers estimate that between 1.7 m and more than 10 m of surface material vanished. This erosion could either expose fresher interior layers or erase the chemical fingerprints of the comet’s birthplace. Knowing how much of the surface has been altered is essential for interpreting the spectra we collected.

<Callout type="takeaway">Solar heating likely removed up to a decade of meters from 3I/ATLAS, reshaping the surface we can study.</Callout>

## What happened
3I/ATLAS, discovered in July 2025, is only the third known interstellar object to pass through the Solar System. As it approached perihelion, solar radiation sublimated volatile ices, creating a coma of gas and dust that was monitored by ground‑based and space telescopes.

Tessa Frincke and Darryl Seligman gathered published measurements of gas loss before and after perihelion, finding that the comet expelled roughly 18–58 billion kg of water, carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide. Translating that mass loss into a change in radius, assuming an initial spherical nucleus 1.3 km in radius and a typical comet density, yields a uniform surface recession of about 1.7–5.2 m.

If dust particles escaped alongside the gas, the effective erosion depth could exceed 10 m. The authors stress that the calculation spreads the loss evenly over the entire surface, so localized patches may have worn away more or less than the average.

## Why it matters
Spectroscopic studies of 3I/ATLAS aim to read the chemical record of another planetary system, but any solar‑driven stripping reshapes that record. A thin eroded layer means that some observed gases may still originate from pristine material, while a deeper loss risks contaminating the signal with processed residues. The result influences how astronomers model the comet’s original composition and how future missions might sample untouched interior material.

## How to think about it
When a new interstellar object appears, start by compiling all gas‑escape rates and any dust‑production estimates. Convert the total mass loss into a radius change using a reasonable bulk density, then compare that depth to the size of the nucleus. Treat the result as an upper bound for surface alteration, and overlay it with models of heterogeneous activity to gauge where pristine material might still survive.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the entire observed coma composition comes from untouched material; solar heating may have altered or removed the outermost layers.</Callout>
