On July 1 2023 the European Space Agency launched Euclid, a wide‑field space telescope designed to chart the dark universe. Its first major data set, released today, delivers images covering an area nearly 10,000 times the size of the full moon, encompassing about 900 million galaxies and 60 million quasars. The survey provides roughly 2.5 trillion pixels of information, amounting to 1.7 million GB of data, and reaches back to galaxies that existed when the universe was only 700 million years old. By delivering this unprecedented view of the cosmos, Euclid is poised to reset the baseline for cosmological measurements of dark matter and dark energy.

<Callout type="takeaway">Euclid’s first data release establishes a new benchmark for mapping the universe’s dark components.</Callout>

## What happened

Euclid was launched on July 1 2023 with a mission to map a third of the sky using a wide‑field camera that sees more than 100 times the area captured by Hubble or JWST in a single exposure, producing roughly 20 new images each day. The telescope’s primary goal is to chart the distribution of dark matter and dark energy through precise measurements of galaxy shapes and positions.

The inaugural data set spans an area almost 10,000 times the size of the full moon, containing about 900 million galaxies and 60 million quasars. In total the release comprises approximately 2.5 trillion pixels and occupies 1.7 million GB of storage, offering a statistical sample that reaches back to epochs when the universe was only 700 million years old—about 5 % of its current age.

Leiden astronomer Koen Kuijken notes that Euclid “produces around 20 new images every day,” while Leiden professor Alessandra Silvestri emphasizes that the mission is designed to help unravel the mysteries of the 95 % of the universe that is dark matter and dark energy.

## Why it matters

The sheer volume and depth of Euclid’s survey provide unprecedented statistical power to test theories of cosmic acceleration and the nature of dark matter. By mapping the large‑scale structure with such fidelity, researchers can tighten constraints on the equation of state of dark energy and probe potential deviations from General Relativity. The data also enable studies of early galaxy formation, informing models of how the first structures emerged when the universe was a fraction of its current age. Consequently, Euclid sets a new standard for future cosmological missions and cross‑survey collaborations.

## How to think about it

Researchers should treat Euclid’s release as a foundational dataset for large‑scale statistical studies rather than a final verdict on cosmology. Begin by cross‑matching Euclid’s galaxy catalog with existing surveys (e.g., DESI, LSST) to validate photometric redshifts and shape measurements. Apply rigorous systematic‑error mitigation pipelines before drawing conclusions about dark‑energy parameters, and consider the dataset’s selection functions when modeling early‑universe populations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat the initial release as a final cosmological verdict; systematic checks and cross‑validation with other surveys are essential.</Callout>
