A powerful sunspot region, designated AR4566, has moved onto the Sun’s central meridian, putting it directly in line with Earth. Over the past 24 hours it generated 18 of the 19 C‑class flares recorded, including a near‑M‑class C9.3 event. Forecasters now assign it a 60 % chance of producing an M‑class flare and a 15 % chance of an X‑class flare today. No Earth‑directed coronal mass ejection has been seen yet, but the geometry makes any eruption highly geoeffective. The situation raises the odds of geomagnetic storms that could affect power grids, satellites, and auroral displays.

<Callout type="takeaway">AR4566’s central‑meridian position makes any strong flare a direct space‑weather threat to Earth.</Callout>

## What happened
AR4566 crossed the Sun’s central meridian on November 10, placing the magnetically complex beta‑gamma‑delta region squarely in a geoeffective slot. During the reporting period it produced 18 of the 19 C‑class flares, the strongest being a C9.3 at 08:34 UTC, with additional C5.3 and C8.6 events on the same day. Forecasters have issued a 60 % probability of an M‑class flare and a 15 % probability of an X‑class flare from this region today.

Other active regions—AR4565, AR4568, and newcomer AR4572—remained comparatively quiet, and coronagraph imagery from SOHO showed no Earth‑directed CMEs associated with the recent flares. Solar wind speeds eased from high to moderate levels, indicating a relatively calm interplanetary medium despite the active sunspot.

## Why it matters
When a flare erupts from a region on the central meridian, any accompanying CME is more likely to travel straight toward Earth, potentially triggering geomagnetic storms. Such storms can induce currents in power grids, degrade satellite communications, and enhance auroral activity at lower latitudes. Even without a CME, intense X‑ray flux from an M‑ or X‑class flare can ionize the upper atmosphere, affecting GPS accuracy and high‑frequency radio links.

## How to think about it
Treat the forecast as a risk indicator rather than a certainty. Monitor NOAA’s Space Weather Prediction Center alerts and be ready to implement mitigation steps—such as reorienting solar panels, adjusting satellite orbits, or reinforcing grid protections—if an M‑ or X‑class flare is reported. For amateur observers, check aurora forecasts and plan viewing windows during the evening hours when the flare‑driven geomagnetic activity peaks.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a flare will automatically generate a CME; many C‑class events remain confined to the solar surface.</Callout>
