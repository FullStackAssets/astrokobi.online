On November 16, 2026, NASA released a new Astronomy Picture of the Day featuring the star‑forming region NGC 7129 captured by the James Webb Space Telescope. The infrared image shows a tapestry of glowing gas, dark dust lanes, and narrow jets blasting from young stars. These jets carve filamentary structures in the surrounding nebula, offering a live view of the forces that sculpt nascent stellar systems. By comparing the brighter, gold‑colored gas on the left with the redder region on the right, astronomers can trace different evolutionary stages within the same cloud. Understanding this interplay helps refine models of how stars, planets, and ultimately life‑bearing environments emerge.

<Callout type="takeaway">Webb’s infrared view of NGC 7129 shows stellar jets actively reshaping their birth clouds.</Callout>

## What happened
The James Webb Space Telescope imaged NGC 7129 in infrared, revealing dozens of young stars embedded in a cloud of gas and dust. The left side of the picture glows gold, indicating slightly older protostars that have begun to heat their surroundings, while the right side appears redder, marking cooler, more deeply embedded objects.

Narrow, linear features pierce the nebula, identified as high‑velocity jets and winds launched from the youngest stars. These outflows interact with the surrounding material, creating the filamentary, rope‑like structures that dominate the red region of the image. The contrast between opaque dust lanes and bright infrared emission highlights how dust both obscures and re‑radiates stellar energy.

## Why it matters
Jets and winds are a key feedback mechanism that can both trigger and suppress further star formation by compressing nearby gas or dispersing it. Observing them in situ lets astronomers test theories about how efficiently clouds convert gas into stars and how early planetary systems might be shaped by such turbulence. The infrared perspective also uncovers hidden populations of protostars that visible light cannot detect, expanding the census of newborn stars in our galaxy.

## How to think about it
When evaluating star‑forming regions, treat jet activity as a diagnostic of the earliest evolutionary phases rather than a definitive cause of cloud disruption. Compare infrared morphology with complementary data—such as radio or sub‑millimeter observations—to disentangle line‑of‑sight effects. Use the presence of jets as a flag for regions where protostellar feedback is actively influencing the environment, and incorporate that into any assessment of star‑formation efficiency.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that jets alone determine the fate of a molecular cloud; magnetic fields, radiation pressure, and large‑scale turbulence also play crucial roles.</Callout>
