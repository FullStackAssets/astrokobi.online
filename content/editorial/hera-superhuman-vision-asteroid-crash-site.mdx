The Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) slammed into Dimorphos at more than 22,000 km/h four years ago, deliberately nudging the moonlet away from its orbit around Didymos. While ground‑based telescopes measured a 33‑minute slowdown in Dimorphos’s orbit, they could not see the crater or the material displaced. ESA’s Hera probe has now entered the Didymos binary system and begun sending back its first close‑up images. Its Asteroid Framing Cameras captured Didymos from 28.5 km away with a resolution of roughly 2.7 m per pixel. The data promises to close the gap between impact models and reality.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hera’s high‑resolution, multi‑spectral imaging finally lets scientists measure the DART impact’s true effect.</Callout>

## What happened

DART’s kinetic impactor struck Dimorphos in September 2022, traveling faster than 22,000 km/h and altering the moonlet’s orbital period by about 33 minutes, a change larger than many pre‑mission predictions. The mission was a one‑way strike, leaving scientists without direct visual confirmation of the crater or ejecta.

In early 2026 Hera arrived at the Didymos system, positioning itself roughly 28.5 km from the primary asteroid. Its Asteroid Framing Cameras delivered black‑and‑white images with a ground‑sample distance of about 2.7 m per pixel, revealing surface features such as the “spinning‑top” shape of Didymos and the redistribution of regolith toward its equator.

Hera’s second imaging suite, the HyperScout H instrument, scans the surface in 25 distinct color bands, effectively performing spectroscopy. By comparing these spectral signatures with laboratory measurements of meteorite samples, scientists can infer the mineralogical composition of both Didymos and Dimorphos.

## Why it matters

Direct, high‑resolution imaging validates and refines the physics models that underpin planetary‑defense strategies, reducing uncertainty for future mitigation missions. Knowing the exact crater size and ejecta distribution informs how momentum is transferred in kinetic impacts. Additionally, compositional data from HyperScout H helps assess whether certain asteroid types are more amenable to deflection, shaping risk‑assessment protocols for Earth‑crossing objects.

## How to think about it

Treat Hera’s dataset as a calibration set for kinetic‑impact simulations: first, map crater dimensions and ejecta patterns; second, match HyperScout spectra to known meteorite analogs; third, feed these empirical parameters into momentum‑transfer models. By iterating this process, policymakers can develop more accurate contingency plans for any future asteroid threat.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the composition of Didymos from the initial color‑band data alone; comprehensive spectroscopic analysis will take months.</Callout>
