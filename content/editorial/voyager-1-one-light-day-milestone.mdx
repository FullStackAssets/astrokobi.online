On 18 November 2026 NASA announced that Voyager 1 has reached a distance of one light‑day from Earth. The probe, launched in 1977, is now about 26 billion kilometres (16 billion miles) away, the distance light travels in 24 hours. This milestone underscores how far human‑made objects can travel and provides a concrete scale for interstellar distances. Communications now take a full day to reach the spacecraft and return.

<Callout type="takeaway">Voyager 1 is now one light‑day from Earth, a concrete reminder of interstellar scale.</Callout>

## What happened
Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in 2012 and has been cruising through interstellar space ever since. As of the latest telemetry it sits roughly 26 billion kilometres from the Sun, which translates to the distance light covers in a single day. The spacecraft continues to transmit measurements of magnetic fields, charged particles, and plasma density, despite having switched off non‑essential systems to conserve power.

The probe also carries the Golden Record, a 12‑inch gold‑plated copper disk encoding sounds and images of Earth. Its power source, a radioisotope thermoelectric generator, is slowly diminishing, yet the mission team has kept the science instruments operating by prioritizing low‑consumption modes.

## Why it matters
Voyager 1 provides the only in‑situ data on the interstellar medium surrounding our solar system, informing models of cosmic radiation and magnetic turbulence that affect future deep‑space navigation. The one‑day signal delay makes the spacecraft a living illustration of the communication challenges any interstellar mission will face. Moreover, the milestone offers a tangible reference point for the immense distances to even the nearest exoplanetary systems, which lie many light‑years away.

## How to think about it
Treat Voyager 1 as a benchmark for longevity and distance when designing future probes. Any mission aiming beyond the heliosphere should plan for autonomous operation, low‑power instrumentation, and delayed communications. Use the one‑light‑day metric as a reference when communicating the scale of interstellar travel to policymakers and the public.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that current spacecraft can be commanded with the same immediacy as near‑Earth missions; the one‑day round‑trip time imposes strict limits on real‑time decision making.</Callout>
