Eight years after launch, the Mercury Imaging X‑ray Spectrometer (MIXS) aboard ESA/JAXA’s BepiColombo spacecraft finally opened its eyes to the cosmos, capturing its first X‑ray image of the Crab Nebula. The observation took place during a 39‑hour campaign on September 11‑12, involving 55 spacecraft slews. By comparing the on‑orbit data with the 2015 laboratory calibration, engineers can assess whether the instrument’s angular resolution has held up during the long cruise. The results come just days before BepiColombo’s planned orbital insertion around Mercury, a critical milestone for the mission.

<Callout type="takeaway">MIXS’s first celestial images confirm the instrument remains sharp after an eight‑year journey.</Callout>

## What happened
The MIXS instrument, built by a team led by the University of Leicester, has traveled with BepiColombo for eight years, monitoring its health en route to Mercury. After the Mercury Transfer Module separated from the joint science orbiters in September, MIXS could finally point at the sky and perform calibration measurements.

During September 11–12 the spacecraft executed 55 slews, spending 39 hours observing the Crab Nebula, a well‑characterized X‑ray source 6,500 light‑years away. By measuring how the nebula’s image spreads across the detector, scientists derived the instrument’s angular resolution, finding it slightly better than the 2015 laboratory measurement.

Emma Bunce, principal investigator of MIXS, noted that the early on‑orbit data are essential for confirming that the spectrometer will meet its science goals once BepiColombo begins its orbital insertion sequence tomorrow.

## Why it matters
MIXS is tasked with mapping Mercury’s surface composition using X‑ray fluorescence; its angular resolution directly impacts the level of geological detail that can be resolved. Validating the instrument after a prolonged cruise reassures the mission team that the data returned from Mercury will be scientifically robust. Moreover, the success demonstrates that complex payloads can retain performance over multi‑year deep‑space journeys, informing the design of future interplanetary missions.

## How to think about it
Treat early on‑orbit calibration as a mandatory checkpoint for any long‑duration payload. Compare space‑based measurements with pre‑flight baselines to quantify any drift, and incorporate those findings into mission planning and data processing pipelines. Stakeholders should allocate contingency resources for additional calibration if performance deviates once the spacecraft encounters the target environment.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the angular resolution measured on the Crab Nebula will be identical when imaging Mercury’s surface.</Callout>
