Astronauts Anil Menon (NASA) and Joshua Kutryk (CSA) concluded a 6‑hour‑59‑minute spacewalk on Nov 5, 2026 by deploying the eighth and final iROSA solar array on the International Space Station. The new 63‑foot (19 m) carbon‑composite boom unfolded without motors, augmenting the station’s legacy panels by as much as 30 percent. The EVA marked the end of a 15‑year effort to replace the original solar wings with higher‑efficiency roll‑out units. This upgrade directly expands the station’s power margin for scientific payloads and future commercial modules. It also demonstrates the reliability of the iROSA design under real‑world conditions.

<Callout type="takeaway">The final iROSA installation gives the ISS a significant power boost and completes a multi‑year solar‑upgrade program.</Callout>

## What happened
Menon and Kutryk began the EVA at 8:36 a.m. EST, retrieving the 750‑pound (340 kg) iROSA from a temporary stowage platform using Canadarm2. After positioning the array on the starboard side of the 3B power channel, they attached the electrical connectors. A shunt‑current anomaly appeared, prompting Mission Control’s spacewalk officer Nichole Stilwell to pause deployment until the station entered Earth’s shadow for safe inspection.

Once the issue was resolved and ground tests confirmed acceptable shunt currents, the crew released two bolts and watched the array self‑unfurl to its full length in about ten minutes. The EVA wrapped up at 3:35 p.m. EST, with the astronauts returning Canadarm2 to its park site and cleaning the work area.

The iROSA series, first launched in 2021, replaces aging photovoltaic wings with lightweight, high‑efficiency panels that generate more electricity per unit area. The final installation brings the total to eight arrays, completing the planned power‑augmentation roadmap.

## Why it matters
Increasing the ISS’s power budget enables longer‑duration experiments, higher‑throughput data downlinks, and the operation of new commercial habitats that may dock in the coming years. A 30 percent boost also provides a safety margin during periods of reduced solar exposure, such as eclipses or solar‑panel degradation. The successful deployment validates the roll‑out technology for future orbital platforms and lunar gateway concepts, where mass and volume constraints are critical.

Stakeholders include NASA, international partners, commercial operators, and the scientific community that relies on stable power for microgravity research. The upgrade may also influence policy decisions about extending the ISS’s operational life beyond its current 2030 target.

## How to think about it
When evaluating power upgrades for orbital platforms, compare the incremental wattage gain against crew EVA time, robotic arm usage, and risk of electrical faults. Prioritize modular, self‑deploying panels that can be installed incrementally to spread workload across multiple missions. Incorporate ground‑based “max‑power” testing to anticipate shunt‑current issues before flight.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt to service or replace solar arrays without full EVA training and real‑time ground support, as high‑voltage components pose serious safety hazards.</Callout>
