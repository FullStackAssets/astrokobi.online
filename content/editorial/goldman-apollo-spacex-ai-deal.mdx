Goldman Sachs and private‑equity firm Apollo Global Management are reportedly in talks to sell their stake in SpaceX’s $40 billion artificial‑intelligence partnership. The deal, which ties SpaceX’s satellite and launch capabilities to a massive AI data‑center build‑out, could change who holds the financial reins of the AI infrastructure boom. With AI capital spending projected at $2.8 trillion in the US and $7 trillion globally by 2030, the transaction sits at the intersection of tech ambition and financial markets.

<Callout type="takeaway">The potential sale signals a shift of AI infrastructure ownership from tech operators to financial investors.</Callout>

## What happened
Goldman Sachs and Apollo are said to be negotiating the sale of their interest in SpaceX’s AI partnership, a deal valued at roughly $40 billion. The partnership was designed to leverage SpaceX’s low‑latency satellite network to power next‑generation AI data centers, linking orbital connectivity with terrestrial compute resources.

The move comes as AI build‑out financing has entered an unprecedented scale. By September 2026, AI capital spending in the United States alone was forecast at $2.8 trillion by 2030, with global spending approaching $7 trillion. The bond market now dominates AI funding, and nine major firms have amassed about $3 trillion in off‑balance‑sheet commitments for AI infrastructure.

## Why it matters
Shifting ownership of a $40 billion AI deal to financial institutions could accelerate capital inflows into AI infrastructure, potentially lowering financing costs for hyperscalers but also increasing market exposure to AI‑related credit risk. The transaction may set a precedent for treating large‑scale AI projects as tradable assets, influencing how future AI ventures are funded and structured. Stakeholders ranging from satellite operators to data‑center developers and investors will feel the ripple effects.

## How to think about it
Treat the sale as a signal that AI infrastructure is maturing into a tradable asset class. Investors should assess the credit quality of AI‑related cash flows, compare bond yields against traditional tech financing, and monitor regulatory scrutiny of large off‑balance‑sheet commitments. For operators, consider diversifying funding sources to avoid over‑reliance on a single financial partner.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the sale guarantees lower borrowing costs for AI projects; market volatility can quickly erode any pricing advantage.</Callout>
