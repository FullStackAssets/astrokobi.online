November’s sky is putting on a show for anyone with a clear view of the horizon. Jupiter and Mars will share the dome, skimming just 1.2° apart on the night of the 17th, while Mercury reaches its greatest elongation in the morning. The Leonid meteor shower peaks overnight, and a supermoon will rise near Aldebaran later in the month. For casual stargazers and seasoned amateurs alike, these alignments create a perfect window for naked‑eye and low‑power observing.

<Callout type="takeaway">In November 2026 the night sky offers a rare close pairing of Jupiter and Mars plus several bright planets visible to the naked eye.</Callout>

## What happened
From the evening of November 17 through the 30th, the planets visible to the naked eye from mid‑latitude Northern Hemisphere sites include Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and Mercury. Mercury’s greatest elongation on the morning of the 17th makes it briefly visible low in the east before sunrise. Jupiter and Mars appear together in the western sky after sunset, separated by only about 1.2 degrees, a separation that will widen over the following nights.

The Leonid meteor shower reaches its maximum activity during the early hours of November 18, offering up to 15 meteors per hour under dark skies. A supermoon rises on the night of November 23‑24 near the bright star Aldebaran and the Pleiades, while the Moon skirts Saturn on the evenings of the 19th‑20th. Later in the month the Moon will pass close to Jupiter, Mars and the star Regulus on the mornings of the 29th‑30th.

## Why it matters
These alignments give educators, photographers, and hobby astronomers a concentrated set of targets that illustrate planetary motion, the mechanics of lunar phases, and the interplay of meteoric streams with Earth’s orbit. Observing bright planets without equipment lowers the barrier to entry, while the close Jupiter‑Mars pairing provides a striking visual that can spark public interest in astronomy. The overlapping events also create natural teaching moments about how celestial bodies move in concert.

## How to think about it
Start by checking a reliable weather forecast for clear, dark skies on the target evenings. Use a planet‑tracking app or a simple star‑chart to locate the planets relative to familiar constellations such as Leo and Gemini. If you’re in a light‑polluted area, plan a short trip to a nearby dark‑sky site for the Leonids and the supermoon. A pair of binoculars can reveal the moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, while a modest telescope will show Saturn’s rings when the Moon is nearby. Remember to give your eyes 10‑15 minutes to adapt to darkness before looking up.

<Callout type="warning">Never point a telescope or binoculars at the Sun while searching for planets; doing so can cause permanent eye damage.</Callout>
