Astronomers using the Murchison Widefield Array in Western Australia have detected a repeating radio flash that blinks like a cosmic lighthouse every 7.9 minutes. The bursts appear for a six‑hour window each day before disappearing for roughly 26 hours. Follow‑up observations pinpointed the source as a binary system composed of a white dwarf and a star that closely resembles our Sun. This discovery adds a fresh member to the family of long‑period radio transients and challenges existing models of stellar magnetism.

<Callout type="takeaway">This binary shows that Sun‑like stars can partner with white dwarfs to generate regular, minute‑scale radio bursts.</Callout>

## What happened
The team scanned the sky with the low‑frequency Murchison Widefield Array, applying a search algorithm designed to catch short, periodic radio spikes. In late 2024 they recorded a series of bright flashes arriving every 7.9 minutes, each lasting only a few seconds. The signal was not continuous; it was confined to a six‑hour segment of each 32‑hour cycle, after which the source went silent for about 26 hours.

By cross‑matching the radio coordinates with optical catalogs, researchers identified a Sun‑like G‑type star at the same location. Spectroscopic data revealed a compact companion whose mass and temperature match those of a typical white dwarf, confirming a binary configuration.

Further analysis of the timing and polarization of the bursts suggests the radio emission arises from magnetic interaction between the white dwarf’s strong field and the stellar wind of its Sun‑like partner, a mechanism distinct from previously known pulsar or flare models.

## Why it matters
The finding expands the taxonomy of long‑period radio transients beyond red‑dwarf/white‑dwarf pairs, showing that more massive, Sun‑like stars can also drive minute‑scale radio bursts. This has implications for our understanding of magnetic coupling in close binaries, the late‑stage evolution of solar‑mass stars, and the population statistics of compact binaries in the galaxy. Because the bursts are predictable, the system can serve as a natural laboratory for testing theories of plasma physics under extreme conditions.

## How to think about it
Treat the system as a magnetospheric interaction engine: first assess the orbital period from the radio cadence, then evaluate the magnetic field strength of the white dwarf and the wind properties of the Sun‑like star. Multi‑wavelength follow‑up—optical spectroscopy for stellar classification, X‑ray observations for accretion signatures, and higher‑frequency radio for pulse morphology—will help disentangle competing models. When encountering similar periodic radio sources, apply this framework before invoking exotic explanations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume every long‑period radio transient follows this exact mechanism; each binary may involve different magnetic or accretion dynamics.</Callout>
