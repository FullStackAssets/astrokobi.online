Tonight’s sky over the mid‑latitude Northern Hemisphere offers a rare planetary lineup that amateur astronomers won’t want to miss. Jupiter and Mars are converging to within 1.2 degrees, while Venus, Saturn, and Mercury are also visible in the early morning and evening windows. At the same time the Leonid meteor shower peaks on the night of November 17 and a supermoon will brush past Aldebaran on November 23‑24. These events give a chance to practice naked‑eye observing and to capture striking photographs before the winter clouds arrive.

## What happened
Jupiter and Mars will appear only 1.2 degrees apart on the evening of November 17, making them a striking pair in the western sky after sunset. Venus remains bright in the early evening, while Saturn follows later, and Mercury reaches its greatest elongation from the morning sun, allowing a brief glimpse before dawn.

The Leonid meteor shower, known for its fast, bright meteors, reaches its peak overnight on November 17, offering observers a chance to see dozens of meteors per hour under dark conditions. Additionally, a supermoon will rise near the bright star Aldebaran on the evenings of November 23‑24, and the Moon will sit close to Saturn on November 19‑20, providing a striking lunar‑planetary conjunction.

Later in the month, the Moon will pass near Jupiter, Mars, and the star Regulus on November 29‑30, and a series of evening charts show the Moon’s proximity to the twin stars of Gemini on November 26‑27, giving multiple opportunities for casual and photographic observing.

## Why it matters
These celestial events are accessible to anyone with a clear horizon, turning a routine night into a teaching moment about orbital mechanics and the solar system’s geometry. Public interest spikes when planets appear close together, which can inspire new hobbyists and support dark‑sky advocacy by highlighting the loss of visibility in light‑polluted areas. Moreover, the Leonid shower and supermoon provide natural laboratories for studying meteoroid streams and lunar illumination effects, data that feed into broader astronomical research.

## How to think about it
Start by checking the local weather and moon phase; a clear, moonless hour after sunset is ideal for the Leonids, while the supermoon is best observed when the sky is still dark enough to see Aldebaran’s orange hue. Use a free planetarium app such as Stellarium to pinpoint exact rise times for each planet and the Moon. If you’re photographing, set a wide aperture and a moderate ISO to capture both bright planets and faint meteors without overexposing the Moon. Finally, record your observations in a simple log—date, time, location, and what you saw—to build a personal sky‑watching archive.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt to view the planets or the Moon near sunrise without proper eye protection; looking directly at the Sun can cause permanent eye damage.</Callout>
