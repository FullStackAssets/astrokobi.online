A recent analysis of data from ESA’s Trace Gas Orbiter has revealed hydrogen chloride leaking from fissures in the Alba Mons region of Mars. Hydrogen chloride is a volcanic gas on Earth, typically released when hot magma interacts with surrounding rocks. Its presence on Mars points to the possibility that magma is still moving beneath the planet’s surface, despite the lack of surface eruptions. If true, the Red Planet would be more geologically active than previously thought, reshaping our models of its thermal evolution.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hydrogen chloride detections suggest Mars may still host active subsurface magmatism.</Callout>

## What happened
The European Space Agency’s Trace Gas Orbiter recorded elevated levels of hydrogen chloride (HCl) directly above narrow fissures in the Alba Mons volcanic province, publishing the findings in Science Advances on November 4, 2026. The instrument’s spectrometer identified the gas as a localized plume, distinct from the background atmospheric composition.

Mars has long been considered a largely dormant world, but the InSight lander previously logged more than 1,300 marsquakes, indicating a still‑active interior. Combined with surface imaging that shows relatively fresh lava flows, the new HCl detection adds a chemical dimension to the growing case for ongoing magmatic processes.

On Earth, HCl is a minor volcanic gas, released when hot magma contacts chloride‑bearing rocks. While water vapor, CO₂, and SO₂ dominate terrestrial eruptions, scientists note that on Mars the thin atmosphere could allow HCl to persist longer, making it a more prominent volcanic tracer.

## Why it matters
If magma is moving beneath the surface, Mars retains a heat source that could influence its atmospheric chemistry, potential subsurface habitats, and the longevity of geothermal gradients. Active volcanism would also affect the interpretation of remote‑sensing data, as transient gas releases could be mistaken for seasonal processes. For future exploration, regions with possible magmatic activity become prime targets for drilling or sample‑return missions seeking clues about the planet’s evolution and habitability.

## How to think about it
Treat the HCl signal as a hypothesis‑driven clue rather than definitive proof of eruptions. Combine it with seismic data, thermal imaging, and high‑resolution topography to build a multi‑parameter model of subsurface activity. When planning missions, prioritize instruments that can detect both gases and heat flux, and consider deploying networked landers or rovers to monitor temporal changes in the Alba Mons area.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume Mars is currently erupting; the evidence is indirect and requires corroboration from additional measurements.</Callout>
