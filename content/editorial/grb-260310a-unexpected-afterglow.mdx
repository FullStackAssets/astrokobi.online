On March 10 2026 the Fermi Gamma‑ray Space Telescope recorded GRB 260310A, a burst whose initial gamma flash lasted almost a minute but released modest energy. Unusually, its optical afterglow grew brighter over several hours, eventually outshining the host galaxy. The event forces astronomers to reconsider how jet energy is transferred to surrounding material.

<Callout type="takeaway">A seemingly dim gamma‑ray burst can become the brightest source in its sky hours after the initial flash.</Callout>

## What happened
Fermi detected a prompt gamma‑ray emission that lasted roughly 55 seconds, shorter than the typical 20‑30 second long GRB but with a lower total fluence. Follow‑up observations with ground‑based optical telescopes recorded a faint afterglow that steadily increased in brightness, peaking about three hours after the trigger and surpassing the luminosity of the host galaxy. X‑ray monitoring showed a standard decay, while radio observations later confirmed the presence of a relativistic jet interacting with the interstellar medium.

The light curve therefore combined a modest, brief gamma flash with a delayed, luminous optical component—behavior not predicted by standard fireball models that assume a monotonic decline after the prompt phase.

## Why it matters
The event challenges the assumption that the prompt gamma energy directly predicts afterglow brightness. If jet composition or viewing angle can produce delayed energy release, distance estimates based on afterglow scaling may need revision. It also blurs the line between long GRBs and other transients, suggesting a broader diversity of jet‑medium interactions that could affect the use of GRBs as cosmological probes.

## How to think about it
Treat GRB 260310A as a reminder that GRB jets can release energy on multiple timescales. When evaluating a new burst, compare prompt fluence with early optical monitoring rather than assuming a simple decay. Incorporate jet‑angle and medium density variations into models, and prioritize simultaneous gamma, X‑ray, optical, and radio coverage to capture unexpected brightening.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume all dim GRBs will show late brightening without supporting multi‑wavelength observations.</Callout>
