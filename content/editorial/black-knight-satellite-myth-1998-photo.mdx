In 1998, astronauts on the Space Shuttle Endeavour captured a photo of a dark, oddly shaped object drifting away from the International Space Station. The object turned out to be a thermal cover that had detached from a trunnion pin during the STS‑88 assembly mission. Because the cover floated silently against the blackness of space, the image was later co‑opted by proponents of the “Black Knight Satellite” conspiracy, who claim it is an ancient alien probe. The story illustrates how a mundane piece of hardware can become a catalyst for a century‑old myth that blends early radio experiments, misread signals, and modern internet folklore.

<Callout type="takeaway">A stray thermal cover, not an alien craft, is the seed of the Black Knight Satellite legend.</Callout>

## What happened
During the final days of the STS‑88 mission, Mission Commander Bob Cabana reported over the radio that “one of the thermal covers got away,” and astronaut Jerry Ross photographed the object as it floated away from the station. The image, later released to the public, showed a dark, irregular shape that some viewers mistook for an unknown satellite.

Proponents of the Black Knight theory argue that the photographed object is evidence of an ancient extraterrestrial satellite, a claim that traces its narrative back to 19th‑century reports of Nikola Tesla detecting mysterious radio signals. Tesla’s speculation that the signals might be alien communications was later woven into the modern myth, creating a loose chronological thread from early radio experiments to a 1998 space‑debris photo.

## Why it matters
The persistence of the Black Knight story highlights how easily ambiguous visual data can be reinterpreted into sensational narratives, especially when combined with historical anecdotes like Tesla’s experiments. Such myths can erode public trust in space agencies, distract from genuine concerns about orbital debris, and amplify misinformation in an era where images spread rapidly online.

## How to think about it
Treat any striking space image as a hypothesis, not a conclusion. Start by checking mission logs, official NASA explanations, and the technical context of the hardware involved. Compare the visual evidence with known debris catalogs before entertaining extraordinary interpretations. When a claim invokes historical figures like Tesla, verify whether the original statements actually pertain to the current phenomenon.

<Callout type="warning">Do not circulate the 1998 photograph as proof of an alien satellite without first consulting the official NASA clarification.</Callout>
