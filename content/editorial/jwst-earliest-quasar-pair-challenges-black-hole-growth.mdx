When the James Webb Space Telescope turned its infrared eye toward the early universe, it uncovered a surprise that has the astronomy community buzzing. The instrument captured the oldest known pair of supermassive black‑hole‑powered quasars, designated J1602+4228AB and nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua. Seen as they were just 900 million years after the Big Bang, each black hole weighs hundreds of millions to billions of Suns. Their existence at such a cosmic dawn challenges the conventional timeline for black hole growth and forces theorists to rethink how the first massive galaxies formed.

<Callout type="takeaway">The discovery shows that supermassive black holes can reach billions of solar masses far earlier than current models predict.</Callout>

## What happened
The JWST survey of high‑redshift galaxies, complemented by follow‑up imaging from the Gemini North telescope, identified a compact quasar pair at a redshift corresponding to 900 million years after the Big Bang. The system, J1602+4228AB, was given the Hawaiian name Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, meaning “Two celestial bodies of fathomless powerful darkness in the brilliance of heat reincarnating energy into the universe.”

Spectroscopic analysis revealed that the two supermassive black holes have estimated masses of roughly 1.5 billion and 400 million solar masses, respectively, and are separated by only about 18,100 light‑years—approximately one‑fifth the diameter of the Milky Way. The team leader, Silvia Onorato of NOIRLab, noted, “There is no definitive theoretical explanation for how a single one of these giant quasars exists so early in the age of our universe, much less two.”

The pair represents the most compact quasar duo observed during the Cosmic Dawn epoch, a period marked by the emergence of the first stars, galaxies, and black holes.

## Why it matters
If supermassive black holes of this size can form within the first billion years, the mechanisms that feed them—such as rapid gas inflow, direct collapse, or exotic physics—must operate far more efficiently than most simulations allow. This challenges the standard hierarchical growth picture, where black holes grow gradually through mergers and steady accretion.

The finding also impacts our understanding of early galaxy formation, because quasars influence their host galaxies through powerful feedback that can regulate star formation. A population of massive, early quasars could reshape the thermal and ionization state of the intergalactic medium during Cosmic Dawn.

## How to think about it
Treat this discovery as a boundary condition rather than a definitive rule. When building or updating models of early black‑hole formation, incorporate a range of growth rates that can accommodate billion‑solar‑mass objects by 900 million years, but also weigh them against the broader population of less massive early quasars. Keep an eye on upcoming JWST deep‑field programs and next‑generation facilities such as the Roman Space Telescope, which will either confirm that Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua is an outlier or reveal a hidden cohort of similar objects.

<Callout type="warning">Current mass and separation measurements carry significant uncertainties; avoid drawing firm conclusions about cosmology until follow‑up spectroscopy refines these values.</Callout>
