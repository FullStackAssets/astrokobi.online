On November 19 2005 the Japanese probe Hayabusa performed the first ever liftoff from the surface of an asteroid, Itokawa. After two tentative touchdowns that sent the spacecraft into safe mode, the probe briefly rose back into space before attempting a second landing. The maneuver proved that a spacecraft could depart a micro‑gravity body, a prerequisite for future sample‑return missions. Its success set the stage for the eventual return of asteroid dust to Earth in 2010.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hayabusa demonstrated that a probe can lift off from an asteroid, confirming a key capability for sample‑return missions.</Callout>

## What happened
Hayabusa was launched by JAXA on May 9 2003 and reached Itokawa on September 12 2005 after a solar storm degraded its solar arrays, limiting ion‑engine power. The probe deployed a small lander that was lost, then released a target marker and attempted its first touchdown on November 19 2005, descending to about 56 feet above the surface before entering safe mode for three hours.

During the first landing the spacecraft bounced, rested for roughly 39 minutes, and then lifted off—the first recorded liftoff from an asteroid. A second touchdown was attempted later, and although the samplers never fired, the mission continued toward its ultimate goal of returning material.

The sample‑return capsule finally re‑entered Earth’s atmosphere in June 2010, delivering dust from Itokawa and confirming that Hayabusa had indeed collected asteroid material despite the earlier uncertainties.

## Why it matters
The ability to launch from a micro‑gravity body validates a core step for any sample‑return architecture, reducing the risk that a probe could become stranded. It also demonstrated that spacecraft can operate with limited power and still perform complex maneuvers, informing the design of later missions like Hayabusa2 and OSIRIS‑REx. By confirming that asteroid material can be retrieved, the mission opened new avenues for studying the early solar system and for future resource utilization.

## How to think about it
Treat asteroid interactions as a series of low‑risk, repeatable steps: approach, touchdown, diagnostics, and departure. Build in generous power margins and robust safe‑mode recovery procedures, and design sampling hardware that can operate after multiple bounces. Use the Hayabusa experience to justify incremental testing on small bodies before committing to full‑scale resource extraction.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a successful touchdown guarantees sample acquisition; verify collection with independent sensors before departing.</Callout>
