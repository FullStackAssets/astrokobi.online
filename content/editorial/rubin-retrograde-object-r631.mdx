On December 2025 the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s survey captured a faint object beyond Neptune that moves opposite to the planets. Designated R631, the body follows a retrograde orbit tilted 142° to the ecliptic and currently sits about 32 astronomical units from the Sun. Its discovery adds a rare member to the trans‑Neptunian population and raises questions about how such high‑inclination objects formed. Because R631 will reach perihelion at 24 AU in 2047, it may become observable as an active comet, offering a unique laboratory for studying the outer solar system.

<Callout type="takeaway">R631 is a distant, retrograde object that challenges our models of solar‑system dynamics.</Callout>

## What happened
Rubin’s alert stream flagged the moving source in data collected from December 2025 through March 2026. The object’s orbital solution shows an inclination of 142°, placing its motion opposite the usual counter‑clockwise direction.

At the time of discovery R631 was 32 AU from the Sun, just beyond Neptune’s 30 AU orbit. Calculations predict a perihelion of 24 AU on 31 October 2047 and an orbital period of roughly 548 years.

Based on its brightness, astronomers estimate a diameter near 100 km, comparable to the large Oort‑cloud comet C/2014 UN271 (Bernardinelli–Bernstein). If it is cometary, outgassing could begin as it approaches perihelion.

## Why it matters
R631’s extreme inclination and distant orbit provide a rare data point for testing how objects are captured into retrograde paths, shedding light on the dynamical history of the outer solar system and the possible exchange between the scattered disk and the Oort cloud. If the body displays cometary activity at perihelion, it will offer a fresh opportunity to study volatile composition far from the Sun, informing models of solar‑system formation and evolution.

## How to think about it
Treat R631 as a sentinel for the outer solar system: monitor its brightness and motion with Rubin’s nightly alerts, incorporate its orbital parameters into simulations of trans‑Neptunian dynamics, and plan targeted observations with large telescopes as its 2047 perihelion approaches. Recognize that its retrograde nature likely points to past gravitational encounters, so use it to refine models of planetary migration and Oort‑cloud perturbations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume R631 poses any impact risk; its perihelion remains far beyond Earth's orbit.</Callout>
