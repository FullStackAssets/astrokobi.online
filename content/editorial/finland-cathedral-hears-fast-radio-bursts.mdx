Fast radio bursts flash across the cosmos in a millisecond, releasing as much energy as the Sun emits in three days.  A composer and a physicist have turned more than 4,500 of those invisible signals, recorded by the CHIME telescope, into audible tones that now echo through Oulu Cathedral in Finland.  The installation, called “The Logos,” plays the sonified bursts on twelve speakers while a choir prepares to perform alongside them on Nov. 21.  By translating radio data into sound, the project invites the public to experience a phenomenon that is otherwise beyond human perception.

<Callout type="takeaway">Turning FRB data into music lets people hear the universe’s most energetic flashes.</Callout>

## What happened
The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) recorded 4,539 bursts from 3,641 sources between 2018 and 2023, vastly expanding the known catalog of fast radio bursts.  Composer Andrew Melchior and MIT physicist Kiyoshi Masui mapped each burst’s frequency and intensity to musical pitch, creating a soundscape that reflects the bursts’ original radio signatures.  Since early April, the sonified collection has been playing daily inside Oulu Cathedral, with the youngest bursts estimated at 50 million years old and the oldest up to 9 billion years old.

## Why it matters
Hearing FRBs bridges the gap between abstract astrophysical data and human experience, fostering broader public interest in high‑energy astrophysics.  The project also demonstrates a novel way to repurpose large data sets for artistic expression, potentially inspiring new outreach methods that make complex science accessible.  Musicians, educators, and scientists can all benefit from this interdisciplinary approach, which highlights the sheer scale and age of cosmic events in a tangible form.

## How to think about it
Treat the cathedral installation as a complementary narrative rather than a data analysis tool.  When you hear a burst, consider its origin—an extreme event billions of light‑years away—and its energy scale, then ask how the musical mapping reflects those properties.  Use the experience as a springboard to explore the underlying science: read about CHIME’s detection methods, the mystery of FRB progenitors, and the role of neutron stars.  Sharing the sound with others can spark conversations that bridge art, physics, and philosophy.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the musical tones represent exact frequencies or timings of the original radio bursts; they are artistic translations designed for human hearing.</Callout>
