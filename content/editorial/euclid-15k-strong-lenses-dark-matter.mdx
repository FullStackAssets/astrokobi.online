The Euclid space telescope, launched in 2023, has delivered its first massive data release, covering roughly five percent of the night sky. Analysis of the 1.7 million GB of observations revealed more than 15,000 new strong‑gravitational‑lens candidates, pushing the total known sample from under a thousand to over sixteen thousand. These cosmic magnifying glasses bend the light of distant galaxies, letting astronomers peer into the invisible scaffolding of dark matter. The unprecedented haul of lenses promises finer maps of dark matter distribution and tighter constraints on the nature of dark energy.

<Callout type="takeaway">Euclid has increased the catalog of strong gravitational lenses by an order of magnitude, opening a new era for dark matter research.</Callout>

## What happened
The first public release from Euclid contains observations of 900 million galaxies and 60 million quasars gathered over 13 months. The dataset, amounting to 1.7 million gigabytes, spans nearly five percent of the sky and was processed by a global consortium of over 2,000 scientists, including more than 30 Canadians.

Using a combination of automated machine‑learning classifiers and visual inspection, the team identified 15,000 candidate strong lenses. Prior to Euclid, the odds of finding a strong lens were about one in ten thousand; the new catalog represents roughly a ten‑fold increase over the pre‑Euclid total of fewer than 1,000 confirmed systems.

## Why it matters
Strong lenses provide direct measurements of the mass distribution in foreground galaxies and clusters, making them powerful probes of dark matter on both galactic and cosmological scales. A larger sample improves statistical power, allowing researchers to test competing dark‑matter models and to refine measurements of the universe’s expansion driven by dark energy. The catalog also creates a rich target list for follow‑up spectroscopy with ground‑based observatories, accelerating the transition from candidate to confirmed lens.

## How to think about it
Treat the 15,000 candidates as a prioritized queue rather than a finished catalog. Start by cross‑matching with existing spectroscopic surveys to flag the most promising systems. Apply lens‑modeling tools to estimate mass profiles and identify outliers that merit deeper follow‑up. Finally, integrate Euclid’s candidates with data from complementary missions (e.g., Rubin Observatory) to build multi‑wavelength lens samples that reduce selection bias.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume every candidate is a true strong lens; spectroscopic confirmation is essential before using them for quantitative dark‑matter studies.</Callout>
