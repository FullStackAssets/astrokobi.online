On November 5, 2026, Expedition 75 flight engineers Anil Menon and Joshua Kutryk completed the eighth and final International Roll‑Out Solar Array (iROSA) on the ISS. The six‑hour‑plus spacewalk saw the 750‑pound array unfurl to its full 63‑foot length, adding up to 30 % more electrical power to the station. This milestone caps a 15‑year upgrade campaign that began with the first iROSA in 2021. With the new arrays in place, the station can support higher‑energy experiments and extend its operational lifespan. The successful deployment also validates the “Max Power” concept NASA is testing on the ground.

## What happened
The EVA began at 8:36 a.m. EST, with Menon and Canadian astronaut Joshua Kutryk retrieving the 750‑pound iROSA from a temporary stowage platform using Canadarm2. They carried the folded array to the starboard side of the 3B power channel, mounted it onto a pre‑installed bracket kit, and began routing the power cables.

During the hookup, Mission Control reported only half of the expected shunt current, prompting the crew to pause the deployment. They waited for the station to enter Earth’s shadow, inspected the connectors, and confirmed proper current flow before releasing the deployment bolts. Once cleared, the array unfurled in about ten minutes without any motor assistance, extending to its full 63‑foot span.

## Why it matters
The additional iROSA increases the station’s solar capacity by roughly 30 %, providing more power for scientific payloads, life‑support systems, and future commercial modules. Higher power margins improve resilience against solar panel degradation and enable longer‑duration missions aboard the ISS. The successful deployment also demonstrates the reliability of roll‑out technology, informing design choices for lunar gateways and deep‑space habitats that will rely on lightweight, high‑efficiency solar solutions.

## How to think about it
When planning future solar upgrades, treat each array as a plug‑and‑play module: verify electrical characteristics on the ground, schedule EVAs around orbital lighting conditions, and allocate contingency time for connector inspections. Power budgeting should incorporate the extra margin to enable higher‑energy experiments without jeopardising existing systems. Finally, capture lessons learned in a reusable checklist to streamline similar upgrades on the Lunar Gateway or Mars transit vehicles.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt roll‑out array deployments without completing full ground shunt‑current validation and shadow‑window planning, as premature releases can jeopardize both hardware and crew safety.</Callout>
