In a drawer at London’s Natural History Museum, a fossil collected on the Isle of Wight in 1924 lay unnoticed for a hundred years. Two University College London paleontologists retrieved the specimen and applied modern CT scanning and 3‑D modeling. Their analysis revealed a previously unknown megaraptoran, which they named Invictibellatrix amberina. The find highlights how legacy collections can still reshape our view of dinosaur diversity.

<Callout type="takeaway">Centennial‑old museum specimens can still yield brand‑new dinosaur species when examined with today’s technology.</Callout>

## What happened

The specimen was part of a batch of roughly one thousand unstudied fossils that arrived at the Natural History Museum in 1924 after being collected by amateur paleontologist Reginald Walter Tooley. It remained in a storage drawer, catalogued but never fully described.

In 2024, researchers Cassius Morrison and Charlie Scherer revisited the drawer, selected the fragmentary skeleton, and scanned it with high‑resolution computed tomography. The CT data were converted into detailed 3‑D models, allowing the team to examine internal bone structures without damaging the fossil.

Their study, published in Royal Society Open Science on October 14, 2026, identified a distinctive hour‑glass‑shaped cranial crest and a combination of limb proportions that did not match any known megaraptoran. The authors formally named the new species Invictibellatrix amberina, honoring the amber‑rich Wessex Formation and the Latin term for “unconquered female warrior.”

## Why it matters

The discovery expands the geographic range of megaraptoran theropods, previously known mainly from South America and Australia, into the Early Cretaceous of Europe. It also demonstrates the scientific value of museum backlogs, encouraging institutions to invest in non‑destructive imaging of archived material. Moreover, the feathered, bipedal predator adds nuance to our picture of Cretaceous ecosystems on the Isle of Wight, suggesting more complex predator‑prey dynamics than previously thought.

## How to think about it

Museums should prioritize digitizing their unstudied collections, starting with specimens that are well‑catalogued but lack modern analysis. Researchers can form interdisciplinary teams—paleontologists, imaging specialists, and data scientists—to share costs and expertise. When a new species is proposed, publishing the raw CT datasets alongside the description enables independent verification and further study.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that every scanned fossil will represent a novel taxon; rigorous comparative anatomy remains essential before naming a new species.</Callout>
