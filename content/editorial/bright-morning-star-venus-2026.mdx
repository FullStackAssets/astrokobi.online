Spotted a brilliant point of light low in the east just before dawn? That “star” is actually the planet Venus, which has just slipped from the evening to the morning sky. After its inferior conjunction on Oct 24, Venus now rises more than 90 minutes before sunrise and will keep rising earlier each day. Its peak brilliance arrives on Nov 29 at magnitude -4.9, making it easily visible even in daylight. Knowing what you’re looking at turns a commuter’s curiosity into a simple, free skywatching lesson.

<Callout type="takeaway">Venus is the brightest morning object right now and will dominate the pre‑dawn sky through December.</Callout>

## What happened
Venus passed between Earth and the Sun on Oct 24, an event called inferior conjunction, which moved it from the western evening horizon to the eastern morning horizon. Since then it has been rising about three minutes earlier each day, and by Dec 17 it will rise roughly 3 hours 50 minutes before sunrise, staying visible in a dark sky for over two hours.

The planet will reach its maximum brightness on Nov 29, shining at magnitude -4.9. That makes it about twelve times brighter than Jupiter and twenty‑five times brighter than Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky. In dark, rural locations its glare can even cast a faint shadow, and it remains visible as a tiny white speck against the blue daylight sky after sunrise.

## Why it matters
For casual observers, Venus offers a striking, easily recognizable target that requires no equipment, encouraging public interest in astronomy. Its prominence highlights the impact of light pollution: urban dwellers may miss it entirely, while those in dark sites can experience a near‑daylight planetary sighting. Culturally, the “morning star” motif resurfaces, echoing historical references to celestial omens and inspiring educational outreach.

## How to think about it
First, check a reliable sky‑chart app for your location and note the time of Venus’s rise. Aim your eyes toward the east‑southeast horizon about an hour before sunrise; the object will appear as a steady, non‑twinkling point of light, brighter than any star. If you have binoculars, you’ll see Venus as a small disc rather than a pinpoint. For the best view, find a dark site away from streetlights and give your eyes a few minutes to adapt. Remember that Venus will remain visible for weeks, so schedule a quick observation during your commute or morning walk.

<Callout type="warning">Do not look directly at the Sun while searching for Venus; wait until the sky is fully dark before scanning the horizon.</Callout>
