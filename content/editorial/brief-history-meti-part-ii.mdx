Since the 1960s, scientists have moved from bouncing radio signals off nearby planets to deliberately beaming messages toward distant stars. Early experiments such as the 1962 Morse Message from the Evpatoria radar demonstrated that our technology could reach interplanetary distances, while later projects like the 1974 Arecibo transmission and the 1999–2003 Cosmic Call series pushed the envelope to true interstellar scales. Each step has been accompanied by fierce debate over the ethics, safety, and scientific value of announcing our presence. Understanding this trajectory helps us gauge where the next generation of METI might head and what responsibilities accompany that power.

<Callout type="takeaway">Our ability to send intentional interstellar messages has grown, but the ethical stakes have grown with it.</Callout>

## What happened
By the late 20th century, SETI transitioned from a hobbyist pursuit to a recognized scientific field, spurred by advances in rocketry and national space programs. The first intentional interplanetary transmission, known as the Morse Message, was broadcast from the Evpatoria Planetary Radar in Crimea between November 19 and 24, 1962, testing the feasibility of directed radio communication beyond Earth.

In 1974, the Arecibo Observatory sent a 3‑minute binary image of humanity toward the globular cluster M13, marking the first deliberate interstellar message. Subsequent projects such as the Cosmic Call series (1999‑2003) and the 2008 “Teen Age Message” from METI International extended the practice to nearby Sun‑like stars, refining encoding techniques and demonstrating that modern transmitters could reach light‑years.

More recently, METI International and other groups have organized coordinated campaigns, using high‑gain antennas to target exoplanet systems identified by missions like Kepler and TESS. These efforts have shifted the conversation from proof‑of‑concept to policy, as the scientific community grapples with the implications of broadcasting our presence on a galactic scale.

## Why it matters
The progression from planetary to interstellar messaging transforms a technical curiosity into a geopolitical and existential issue. Proponents argue that active messaging could accelerate the discovery of extraterrestrial intelligence, providing a catalyst for scientific and cultural advancement. Critics warn that broadcasting our location may expose humanity to unknown risks, especially if hostile civilizations exist.

Stakeholders range from astronomers and engineers to ethicists, policymakers, and the public. The debate influences funding decisions for large radio facilities, shapes international protocols, and informs how we present humanity to any potential listeners. As transmission power and targeting precision improve, the moral responsibility of each message becomes increasingly significant.

## How to think about it
Treat each METI proposal as a policy decision rather than a purely scientific experiment. Start by asking: What is the specific scientific or cultural goal? Who will be consulted, and how will dissenting voices be incorporated? Evaluate the technical feasibility, the target star’s characteristics, and the message’s content for clarity and longevity. Finally, align the project with existing international agreements or seek to create transparent, multilateral oversight before transmitting.

<Callout type="warning">Do not launch a high‑power transmission without broad scientific, ethical, and governmental review, as premature broadcasting could have irreversible consequences.</Callout>
