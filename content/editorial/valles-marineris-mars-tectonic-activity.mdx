The towering Valles Marineris, the solar system’s largest canyon, is not a frozen relic. A marsquake recorded by NASA’s InSight lander in August 2021 originated beneath the canyon, showing that its deep‑seated faults can still slip. This seismic signal, identified as event S0976a, provides the first direct evidence that the canyon’s tectonic system remains active billions of years after its formation. Understanding that Mars is still reshaping itself reshapes our view of planetary evolution and future exploration.

<Callout type="takeaway">Valles Marineris continues to experience tectonic slip, proving Mars is not entirely geologically dead.</Callout>

## What happened
Scientists from Columbia University and Imperial College London analyzed the S0976a marsquake, the only event recorded by InSight that arrived from the planet’s far side. The waveform and travel‑time modeling pinpointed the source to a fault network deep beneath Valles Marineris, confirming that the canyon’s crustal fractures are still capable of releasing stress.

Valles Marineris stretches roughly 3,900 km, spans up to 600 km in width, and plunges more than 9 km deep—making it orders of magnitude larger than Earth’s Grand Canyon. The new findings indicate that faults created during the canyon’s formation billions of years ago have not hardened completely and can still rupture under present‑day stresses.

## Why it matters
Active tectonics on Mars challenges the long‑standing view of the planet as a dead world and suggests that internal heat and stress are still sufficient to drive crustal movement. This has direct implications for future lander and rover missions, which must consider seismic hazards when selecting sites. Moreover, the discovery expands the search for ongoing geological activity on other seemingly inert planetary bodies.

## How to think about it
Treat planetary surfaces as dynamic until proven otherwise. Incorporate seismic monitoring into mission architectures, even for worlds previously labeled “inactive.” Use Valles Marineris as a case study for evaluating fault‑related risks and for prioritizing regions where subsurface water or heat might persist.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume a single marsquake proves widespread activity; additional observations are needed before revising global tectonic models.</Callout>
