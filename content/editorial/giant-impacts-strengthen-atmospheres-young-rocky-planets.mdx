Young rocky planets have long been thought to lose their thin hydrogen envelopes when they suffer giant impacts. A new study in Nature Astronomy challenges that view, showing that the added mass from a collision can actually tighten a planet’s grip on its atmosphere. By balancing the heat generated against the increase in gravitational binding, collisions may help planets retain primordial gases. This insight reshapes how we interpret the abundance of super‑Earths and sub‑Neptunes discovered beyond our solar system.

<Callout type="takeaway">Giant impacts can increase a planet’s gravitational binding enough to preserve its early atmosphere.</Callout>

## What happened
Researchers Jun Korenaga of Yale and Darius Modirrousta‑Galian, now at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, combined previous work on atmospheric escape and interior evolution to model the aftermath of a giant impact on a young rocky world. Their simulations tracked how the impact adds mass, raises the planet’s binding energy, and partitions the released energy between the mantle and the metallic core of the projectile.

The results indicate that the added mass raises the planet’s gravitational hold on any surrounding gas, while only a fraction of the impact energy translates into mantle heating that could drive atmospheric loss. Consequently, many collisions that were assumed to strip atmospheres may instead reinforce them, especially for planets at or above Earth size.

The study also offers an explanation for the observed dichotomy between super‑Earths, which lack thick primordial envelopes, and sub‑Neptunes, which retain them. The balance between impact‑driven heating and increased binding energy appears to dictate which side of the divide a planet falls on.

## Why it matters
If giant impacts can preserve atmospheres, models of planetary formation must account for a more nuanced interplay between collisional growth and atmospheric retention. This affects estimates of how common habitable‑zone worlds might be, since retaining a hydrogen‑rich envelope can influence surface conditions and volatile delivery. It also guides the interpretation of exoplanet density measurements, helping astronomers distinguish between truly stripped cores and those that simply appear dense due to observational limits.

## How to think about it
When evaluating an exoplanet’s atmospheric prospects, consider its likely collisional history alongside its mass and orbital distance. A planet that has undergone one or more major mergers may have a stronger gravitational well than its current mass alone suggests. Incorporate estimates of binding energy and post‑impact thermal evolution into habitability frameworks rather than assuming impacts always strip gases.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume every giant impact will preserve an atmosphere; outcomes depend on impact size, angle, and the planet’s pre‑impact thermal state.</Callout>
