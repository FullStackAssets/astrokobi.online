On November 14, 2026, SpaceX is set to attempt the first‑ever recovery of a Starship upper stage using the launch tower’s “chopsticks” arms. The vehicle lifted off on Flight 15 the previous evening, placing the Starship stage into orbit and deploying a batch of next‑gen Starlink satellites. After a controlled splashdown of the Super Heavy booster, mission control has kept the upper stage on a trajectory that brings it back to Starbase for a potential catch. If successful, it would mark the first complete orbital rocket recovery, a milestone for reusability. The attempt is being livestreamed for the public.

<Callout type="takeaway">If SpaceX catches the Starship upper stage, it will achieve the first full‑cycle recovery of an orbital rocket.</Callout>

## What happened
SpaceX launched Starship on its 15th test flight from Starbase in South Texas on the night of November 13. The Super Heavy booster performed a controlled splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico 6.5 minutes after liftoff after an automatic abort triggered by a guidance‑navigation issue, while the Starship upper stage continued to orbit and released 26 Starlink V3 satellites.

Mission controllers have scheduled a “chopsticks” catch for the upper stage around 4:20 p.m. EST (2120 GMT) on November 14, roughly 22.5 hours after launch. The tower’s two robotic arms will extend to intercept the stage as it descends, aiming to bring both stages back to the launch pad for refurbishment.

## Why it matters
A successful double‑catch would demonstrate that an entire orbital rocket can be recovered, dramatically lowering launch costs and accelerating the timeline for fully reusable spacecraft. It would also validate the engineering of the tower’s “chopsticks” system, a critical component for SpaceX’s vision of rapid, turn‑key launch operations. Conversely, a failure would highlight the technical challenges that still separate concept from routine practice, influencing investor confidence and regulatory scrutiny.

## How to think about it
Treat the chopsticks attempt as a proof‑of‑concept experiment rather than a guaranteed operational procedure. Evaluate the outcome on three axes: technical success (did the arms secure the stage?), hardware condition (post‑catch inspection results), and schedule impact (how quickly can the stage be turned around). Use this framework to gauge how quickly full‑cycle reusability might become a routine part of launch operations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a successful catch means immediate commercial availability of fully reusable Starship flights; extensive refurbishment will still be required.</Callout>
