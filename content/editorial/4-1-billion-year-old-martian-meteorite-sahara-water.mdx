The Sahara Desert yielded a 4.1‑billion‑year‑old rock that scientists have confirmed originated on Mars. Named Teghaza 001, the 800‑gram meteorite was blasted off the Red Planet long before the planet lost most of its surface water. Its mineral mix mirrors materials seen in Mars’ southern highlands, suggesting it formed in a region never before sampled. Understanding how water interacted with this rock could rewrite models of early Martian climate and habitability.

<Callout type="takeaway">Teghaza 001 is the oldest known piece of Martian crust that records direct evidence of water alteration.
</Callout>

## What happened
Researchers identified Teghaza 001 in a Mali salt plain in 2022 and used oxygen‑isotope signatures to confirm its Martian origin. Advanced rubidium‑strontium dating of embedded zircon crystals placed its formation over 4.1 billion years ago, making it one of the three oldest Martian meteorites ever discovered. The rock’s unique blend of igneous minerals and textures preserves signs that liquid water altered it shortly after it formed, a finding supported by mineral similarities to observations from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.

The team outlined thirteen possible source regions on Mars where the meteorite could have originated, but the exact location remains uncertain. Nonetheless, the presence of water‑altered minerals indicates a dynamic hydrologic cycle on early Mars, long before the planet’s atmosphere thinned.

## Why it matters
If water interacted with the Martian crust this early, it expands the window during which habitable conditions might have existed. The meteorite provides a tangible sample of crustal material that can be studied in Earth‑based labs, offering insights that remote sensing alone cannot deliver. This evidence could influence future mission planning, prioritizing landing sites that match the meteorite’s mineral signature.

## How to think about it
Treat Teghaza 001 as a “time capsule” that complements orbital data. Compare its mineralogy with spectra from the southern highlands to narrow down its source region. Use isotopic analyses to trace water sources and quantify how much water was present. Incorporate these findings into broader climate models to test scenarios of early Martian habitability. Finally, consider the meteorite when evaluating future sample‑return targets, as it demonstrates the value of ancient crustal material.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the meteorite’s water signatures represent the entire planet; its limited provenance means broader extrapolations require caution.
</Callout>
