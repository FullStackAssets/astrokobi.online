Stars forge oxygen, the universe's third most abundant element, but new Hubble observations show that tiny dwarf galaxies keep only a fraction of what they produce. Researchers examined 37 nearby dwarf galaxies within 22 million light‑years, comparing the total oxygen expected from their stellar populations with the amount still present in stars, gas, and dust. The analysis reveals that these galaxies retain between 4 % and 25 % of their oxygen, averaging about 11 %. This loss reshapes our picture of how metals circulate between galaxies and the intergalactic medium.

<Callout type="takeaway">Dwarf galaxies lose roughly 90 % of the oxygen their stars create.</Callout>

## What happened
Scientists used Hubble’s spectroscopic instruments to measure oxygen emission lines in 37 dwarf galaxies, the most extensive sample of its kind within 22 million light‑years of Earth. By combining these measurements with models of stellar evolution, they estimated the total oxygen each galaxy’s stars should have produced over billions of years. Comparing that estimate to the observed oxygen in stars, gas, and dust showed a retention range of 4 %–25 %, with an average of about 11 %.

The study also examined the role of galactic winds—streams of gas driven outward by supernova explosions and massive stellar winds. The observed oxygen deficit suggests these winds eject far more metal‑rich material than current simulations predict, especially in shallow gravitational potentials typical of dwarf systems.

## Why it matters
Oxygen serves as a tracer for the broader cycle of heavy elements (metals) that shape galaxy evolution, star formation, and the chemical makeup of the universe. If dwarf galaxies routinely lose the majority of their metals, the intergalactic medium becomes enriched earlier, influencing the material that later‑forming, larger galaxies accrete. The findings also expose a gap between theoretical models of feedback processes and actual observations, prompting revisions to how simulations handle wind efficiency.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a galaxy’s chemical budget, start with the stellar oxygen production estimate, then subtract the observed oxygen in all baryonic components. The residual represents the metal mass expelled by winds. Compare this residual fraction across galaxy masses to gauge wind efficiency, and use it to calibrate feedback parameters in numerical models. Remember that deeper potential wells in larger galaxies will retain a higher fraction, so scaling relations must account for gravitational binding energy.

<Callout type="warning">Do not extrapolate these retention rates to massive galaxies without accounting for their deeper gravitational wells.</Callout>
