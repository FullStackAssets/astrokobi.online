The gamma‑ray burst GRB 260310A lit up the sky on March 10, 2026 with a surprisingly modest flash that lasted about 53 seconds. Despite its low‑energy prompt emission, the event produced one of the brightest afterglows recorded in two decades, outshining its host galaxy in visible light. An international team followed the fading light across X‑ray, optical, and radio wavelengths, uncovering a supernova signature and a prolonged X‑ray rebrightening weeks later. The contrast between a quiet prompt flash and a dazzling afterglow forces astronomers to reconsider how viewing angle shapes what we see.

<Callout type="takeaway">The bright afterglow shows the burst was an off‑axis jet, not a weak explosion.</Callout>

## What happened
The burst, designated GRB 260310A, was caught by NASA’s Fermi Gamma‑ray Space Telescope on March 10, 2026. Its initial gamma‑ray flash lasted roughly 53 seconds and appeared to release relatively little energy compared with typical long‑duration bursts.

A coordinated campaign of ground‑ and space‑based observatories then tracked the event from X‑rays to radio. NASA’s Chandra and ESA’s XMM‑Newton monitored the high‑energy emission, while the Large Binocular Telescope captured the optical rise that surpassed the host galaxy’s brightness over several hours. Radio observations with the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array and additional data from the Einstein Probe and Calar Alto Observatory revealed a supernova signature, confirming the death of a massive star.

Putting the pieces together, the team concluded that Earth observed the jet from an off‑axis perspective. The modest prompt flash reflects the narrow, intensely beamed core missing our line of sight, while the later, broader afterglow arises as the jet’s slower, wider material spreads into view.

## Why it matters
If many gamma‑ray bursts are viewed off‑axis, the true energy budget of these explosions could be far larger than inferred from prompt gamma‑ray measurements alone. This realization reshapes population studies, suggesting that the observable sample may be biased toward narrowly beamed events. Understanding jet geometry also informs models of how massive stars die and how their jets interact with surrounding material, impacting predictions for associated gravitational‑wave and neutrino signals.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a gamma‑ray burst, first assess the prompt emission’s brightness and duration, then map the afterglow’s evolution across the spectrum. If the afterglow outshines the prompt phase, consider an off‑axis geometry and look for supernova signatures that confirm a massive‑star origin. Use coordinated observations to capture early optical rise and late‑time X‑ray rebrightening, which together constrain jet opening angles and viewing direction.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume a bright afterglow automatically means an off‑axis jet; other factors like dense circumburst media can also enhance late‑time emission.</Callout>
