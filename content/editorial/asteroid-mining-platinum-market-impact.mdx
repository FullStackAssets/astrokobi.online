Asteroid mining is moving from science‑fiction to a market‑shaping reality. A recent MIT systems‑dynamics study models what would happen if a startup like AstroForge began returning platinum‑group metals from metallic asteroids. The model predicts a sudden surge of supply that could drive platinum prices sharply lower. Because PGMs underpin catalytic converters, hydrogen fuel cells, and other clean‑energy technologies, a price collapse could accelerate adoption. Understanding this scenario helps investors, policymakers, and engineers prepare for a new resource paradigm.

<Callout type="takeaway">A flood of asteroid‑sourced platinum could collapse prices, making the metal cheaper for green technologies.</Callout>

## What happened
System dynamicists at MIT published a paper titled “Will AstroForge Collapse the PGM Market?” that simulates the economic impact of returning large quantities of platinum‑group metals from space. The study notes that more than 80% of known planetary reserves of PGMs are locked in deep‑shaft mines in South Africa and Russia, where extraction costs run into tens of millions of dollars per ton and yields are only a few grams per ton of rock.

Metallic (M‑type) asteroids are the exposed cores of protoplanets and contain PGMs at concentrations dozens of times higher than Earth’s richest mines. A single kilometer‑wide asteroid is estimated to hold more PGMs than humanity has extracted throughout recorded history. AstroForge, a California‑based startup, is planning to harvest these materials and deliver them to Earth’s market, directly challenging the terrestrial supply chain.

## Why it matters
If asteroid‑derived PGMs flood the market, platinum prices could drop dramatically, lowering the cost of catalytic converters and hydrogen fuel cells and speeding the transition to low‑carbon transportation. Cheaper PGMs also reduce the economic incentive for illegal theft of catalytic converters, a persistent problem in many regions. At the same time, a rapid price decline could destabilize existing mining companies, reshape geopolitical power tied to mineral exports, and trigger a re‑evaluation of resource‑security strategies worldwide.

## How to think about it
Treat asteroid‑derived PGMs as a disruptive supply shock rather than a steady supplement. Track price trends and adjust procurement strategies accordingly. Diversify supply chains by investing in recycling and alternative catalyst materials. Engage with policy makers to shape clear, internationally agreed rules for space resource extraction, ensuring that commercial activity aligns with sustainability goals.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume asteroid mining will instantly solve all supply‑chain challenges; the technology, launch costs, and regulatory framework remain uncertain and could delay market entry.</Callout>
