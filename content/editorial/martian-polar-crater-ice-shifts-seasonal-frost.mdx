A new study of Mars’ north polar region shows that seasonal frost actively reshapes ice deposits within most craters. Researchers examined high‑resolution images of 36 craters taken by the HiRISE camera between 2006 and 2025 (Mars Years 28–38). Bright‑reflectance changes were detected in 32 of those craters, indicating that ice expands during the winter cold and retreats in the summer heat. These findings provide a rare glimpse of present‑day climate processes on the Red Planet.

<Callout type="takeaway">Seasonal frost reshapes ice deposits in most Martian polar craters, revealing active climate processes.</Callout>

## What happened
The team compiled a time‑series of HiRISE observations for each crater, comparing images taken at different points in the Martian year.
In 32 craters the surface brightness shifted in a pattern consistent with frost deposition in winter and sublimation in summer, while the remaining four showed little or no change.
The seasonal cycle was most pronounced on crater floors that sit below the surrounding ice cap, suggesting that local topography amplifies frost accumulation.

## Why it matters
Active ice movement demonstrates that Mars’ polar climate is not static but continues to evolve on decadal timescales, a factor that must be incorporated into climate models. Understanding these cycles helps reconstruct past atmospheric pressure and temperature trends, which are essential for assessing the planet’s habitability history. Future landers and rovers targeting water ice resources can use crater‑scale frost behavior to refine site selection and mission planning.

## How to think about it
When evaluating Martian polar data, treat ice deposits as seasonally variable rather than permanent, and cross‑reference crater observations with orbital climate models to capture local effects. Scientists and mission planners should factor in the timing of frost cycles when estimating water availability, especially for missions that rely on in‑situ resource utilization. A useful framework is to map observed changes onto the Martian seasonal calendar, then assess how those patterns scale to larger regions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the observed crater ice cycles apply uniformly across the entire polar ice cap.</Callout>
