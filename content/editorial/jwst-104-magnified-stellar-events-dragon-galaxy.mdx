The James Webb Space Telescope has now catalogued 104 transient, highly magnified stellar events inside the so‑called Dragon galaxy, a massive system at redshift 0.725. These events were uncovered by comparing JWST images taken between 2022 and 2024, revealing sudden brightenings that last days to weeks. Because each flash is amplified by gravitational lensing along micro‑caustics, they provide a rare glimpse of individual stars in a galaxy 6.5 billion light‑years away. The new tally more than doubles the previous record of 44 events in the same arc, sharpening a tool that astronomers hope will also expose the distribution of dark matter in the foreground cluster.

<Callout type="takeaway">JWST’s detection of 104 lensed stellar flares doubles the known sample, turning the Dragon arc into a powerful laboratory for dark‑matter studies.</Callout>

## What happened
Using JWST’s NIRCam, a team led by José María Palencia compared deep exposures of the Dragon arc taken in 2022, 2023 and 2024. By aligning the images and subtracting them pixel‑by‑pixel, they flagged 104 locations where the flux rose dramatically and then faded over days to weeks. Each event was verified against archival data to rule out instrumental artifacts, confirming genuine astrophysical transients.

The Dragon galaxy sits at redshift 0.725 behind the massive galaxy cluster Abell 370. The cluster’s gravity stretches the background galaxy into a luminous arc, and tiny irregularities—stars and possibly dark‑matter clumps—in the cluster create “microcaustics.” When a background star drifts across one of these microcaustics, its two lensed images merge, boosting its brightness by factors of hundreds to thousands for a brief interval.

The findings were published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* on September 30, 2026. The authors note that the observed magnifications, lasting from a few days to several weeks, match theoretical expectations for caustic‑crossing events and provide a statistical sample large enough to begin probing the mass spectrum of the intervening dark matter.

## Why it matters
These detections turn the Dragon arc into a natural telescope that can resolve individual stars at cosmological distances, a regime otherwise inaccessible. By measuring how often and how strongly stars are magnified, astronomers can infer the granularity of the lensing cluster’s mass distribution, offering indirect constraints on the nature of dark matter subhalos. The enlarged sample also refines estimates of the high‑mass end of the stellar initial mass function in a galaxy observed when the universe was less than half its current age. Consequently, the work informs both galaxy‑evolution models and particle‑physics theories that predict dark‑matter clumping.

## How to think about it
Treat each caustic‑crossing event as a data point in a statistical experiment rather than a standalone discovery. When planning future surveys, prioritize fields with known strong lenses and allocate regular cadence observations to capture the short‑lived peaks. Combine JWST photometry with detailed mass models of the foreground cluster—updated with spectroscopic redshifts of cluster members—to translate magnification factors into constraints on dark‑matter clump masses. Finally, integrate these results with simulations of stellar populations at similar redshifts to test whether the observed flare rates match theoretical expectations.

<Callout type="warning">Magnification estimates are highly sensitive to the assumed lens model; over‑interpreting a single event’s brightness can lead to misleading dark‑matter conclusions.</Callout>
