When the This Week In Space podcast aired its 236th episode, host Tariq Malik invited astrobiologist Mikhail Tikhonov to challenge our picture of alien life. The conversation moved beyond the familiar sci‑fi tropes of carbon‑based microbes and humanoid beings. Tikhonov argued that any system that captures energy, transforms it, and replicates should count as life. If that definition holds, whole classes of exotic chemistry could be considered alive. The shift has immediate implications for how we design future missions and interpret ambiguous signals.
<Callout type="takeaway">Life may be far stranger than carbon‑based microbes, so we must broaden our detection criteria.</Callout>
## What happened
In the hour‑long episode, Tariq Malik and co‑host Rod Pyle introduced Tikhonov, a researcher at Washington University, who outlined a minimalist definition of life: an entity that takes in energy, transforms it, and replicates. He illustrated the idea with examples ranging from self‑replicating crystal lattices to hypothetical silicon‑based metabolisms, emphasizing that such systems would be invisible to traditional carbon‑centric biosignature surveys.
The hosts linked the discussion to current space activities, noting that SpaceX’s upcoming Starship launch provides a rare chance to test wide‑field instruments that could capture transient energy signatures. While the episode also featured brief segments on telescope recommendations and model rockets, the core takeaway remained the need to rethink what we consider “alive” when scanning other worlds.
## Why it matters
If life can exist in chemistries we do not yet recognize, the pool of potentially habitable environments expands dramatically—from icy moons with subsurface oceans to rocky exoplanets with thick atmospheres of exotic gases. This broader view forces mission planners to equip probes with more versatile analyzers, and it pushes SETI researchers to develop signal‑processing techniques that look for patterns beyond traditional radio pulses. Ultimately, redefining life reshapes funding priorities and public expectations about finding extraterrestrials.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a new observation, first ask whether the signal or chemistry fits the energy‑capture‑transform‑replicate template, regardless of its elemental makeup. Next, compare the finding against known abiotic processes to rule out false positives. Finally, incorporate interdisciplinary expertise—chemists, physicists, computer scientists—to model how such exotic life could sustain itself over geological timescales.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that any energy‑transforming system we detect is automatically biological; rigorous validation is essential.</Callout>
