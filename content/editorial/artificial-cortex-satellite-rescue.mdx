The low‑Earth orbit is becoming crowded, with almost 20,000 active satellites and more than 3,500 non‑functional “zombie” units that cannot signal their presence. Researchers at the University of Illinois have unveiled CORTEX, an autonomous, convex‑based framework that designs and guides close‑range rendezvous trajectories for satellite repair missions. By automating the rendezvous and proximity operations that were once bespoke, CORTEX could turn ad‑hoc rescue flights into routine services. If successful, the system would help prevent collisions that could trigger a Kessler‑type cascade of debris.
<Callout type="takeaway">CORTEX aims to make satellite rescue an automated, scalable service, reducing collision risk in an increasingly congested LEO.</Callout>
## What happened
The orbital environment has shifted dramatically: tracked objects in LEO rose from about 13,700 in 2019 to over 24,000 in 2025, and the population of dead or unresponsive satellites now exceeds 3,500. These “zombies” pose a hidden danger because they cannot broadcast collision‑avoidance data, increasing the probability of accidental impacts.

In response, a team led by Minduli Wijayatunga published a paper in Acta Astronautica describing CORTEX, an end‑to‑end convex optimization framework that generates safe, fuel‑efficient rendezvous trajectories and commands a hardware testbed to execute them. The system integrates perception, planning, and guidance into a single autonomous loop, allowing a servicer spacecraft to approach, dock, and repair a target without human‑in‑the‑loop decisions.

Initial hardware experiments have demonstrated sub‑meter accuracy in close‑range navigation and successful execution of a mock repair maneuver, providing the first experimental validation of a fully autonomous RPO pipeline for satellite servicing.
## Why it matters
The ability to autonomously service or de‑orbit zombie satellites could dramatically reduce the risk of cascade collisions that would otherwise generate thousands of new debris fragments. By turning rescue missions into repeatable, low‑cost operations, CORTEX also lowers the economic barrier for satellite operators to maintain constellation health, preserving the utility of LEO for communications, Earth observation, and scientific research.

## How to think about it
Treat CORTEX as a modular service layer: first assess the target satellite’s physical accessibility, then let the autonomous planner generate a collision‑free trajectory, and finally verify the plan with ground‑based safety analysis before execution. Operators should integrate CORTEX outputs into existing traffic‑management tools rather than replace them outright.
<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on CORTEX for critical missions until extensive on‑orbit testing confirms its safety and reliability.</Callout>
