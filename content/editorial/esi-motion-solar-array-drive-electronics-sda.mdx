On Oct. 30, 2026, ESI Motion announced it will supply its Solar Array Drive Electronics (SADE) to Lockheed Martin for the Space Development Agency’s Warfighter Space Architecture. The SADE units—based on the company’s SuperNova and Pulsar motor controllers—are designed to keep satellite solar arrays precisely oriented in orbit. By integrating these components, the SDA aims to field a resilient constellation that can track missiles and provide secure communications for U.S. forces. The deal marks a notable step in marrying commercial space‑hardware expertise with national‑defense satellite programs.

<Callout type="takeaway">ESI’s SADE will enable the SDA’s satellite network to maintain power and pointing precision for missile‑defense missions.</Callout>

## What happened
The agreement, disclosed in a press release, names ESI Motion as the supplier of SADE hardware for Lockheed Martin’s work on the SDA’s Warfighter Space Architecture. The SADE system includes flight‑heritage motor controllers that drive solar‑array panels, providing fine‑grained attitude control and high‑reliability power management.

ESI, a U.S. company with over two decades of experience serving space, military, and industrial customers, highlights that its SuperNova and Pulsar controllers have already flown on multiple classified and commercial missions. The company’s marketing materials note that the technology is space‑rated and designed for long‑duration operations.

## Why it matters
Power and attitude control are the lifelines of any satellite, especially those tasked with continuous missile‑tracking and secure communications. By leveraging proven commercial hardware, the SDA can accelerate deployment while reducing development risk, potentially fielding a larger, more distributed constellation faster than a purely government‑built solution. This partnership also signals a broader trend of integrating private‑sector expertise into critical defense architectures, raising the strategic stakes of supply‑chain security.

## How to think about it
When evaluating similar defense‑space contracts, weigh the trade‑off between rapid fielding and supply‑chain resilience. Ask whether the commercial component’s heritage aligns with mission‑critical performance thresholds, and map out contingency plans if the supplier faces disruptions. Incorporate independent testing regimes to verify that the hardware meets classified security standards before integration.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that commercial space hardware automatically satisfies all classified security requirements; rigorous vetting is still essential.</Callout>
