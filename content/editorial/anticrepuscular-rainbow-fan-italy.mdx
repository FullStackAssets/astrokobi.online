At sunset last week, a photographer in Presenzano, Italy, captured a striking fan of colors that looked like a rainbow hanging in front of the setting sun. The band is incomplete and curves upward, opposite the usual arc we associate with rainbows. This illusion is caused by anticrepuscular rays, where sunlight passes through gaps in clouds and converges near the horizon opposite the sun. The image was posted to NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day on October 20, 2026, drawing attention to a rarely seen atmospheric trick. Understanding it deepens public appreciation of how light and water interact on a planetary scale.

<Callout type="takeaway">Anticrepuscular rays can make a rainbow appear in front of the sun, turning the sky into a reverse rainbow fan.</Callout>

## What happened
The NASA APOD entry shows a phone‑taken photograph of an incomplete rainbow‑shaped fan above the hills of Presenzano, Italy, taken just after sunset. The photographer noticed the unusual band while hanging laundry, prompting a quick capture that revealed the phenomenon.

Anticrepuscular rays occur when sunlight streams through gaps in low clouds and travels across the entire sky, converging toward the point opposite the sun. Because rainbows are formed by backward reflection of sunlight from raindrops, the sun must be behind the observer; in this case the rays create the illusion that the sun is in front, producing a reverse‑rainbow fan.

## Why it matters
The event illustrates how everyday atmospheric conditions can generate spectacular optical effects, offering a tangible teaching moment for educators and a reminder that the sky’s visual language is still full of surprises. It also underscores the value of citizen science: a casual phone snapshot contributed to a NASA feature, inspiring curiosity about atmospheric physics. For the broader public, such images reinforce the connection between local weather and the larger dynamics of Earth’s atmosphere.

## How to think about it
When you see a rainbow‑like band near the horizon opposite the sun, ask: are there gaps in low clouds? Is the sun setting or rising? Consider the geometry—rainbows require the sun behind you, so a band that appears in front signals anticrepuscular rays. Use the observation as a prompt to explore light scattering, refraction, and the scale of atmospheric layers.

<Callout type="warning">Never look directly at the sun while trying to locate anticrepuscular rays; use indirect viewing methods to protect your eyes.</Callout>
