A new binary system has been pinpointed as the source of a mysterious radio beacon that flashes like clockwork every 7.9 minutes. The system pairs a white dwarf—the dense remnant of a Sun‑mass star—with a main‑sequence star that closely matches our Sun in temperature and size. The discovery came from a targeted search with the Murchison Widefield Array in Western Australia, which recorded bursts that appear for six‑hour windows before disappearing for roughly a day. This finding adds a Sun‑like companion to the roster of long‑period radio transients, which previously involved only red dwarfs. Understanding the mechanism could reshape how astronomers interpret intermittent radio signals across the galaxy.
<Callout type="takeaway">A white dwarf paired with a Sun‑like star can generate regular 8‑minute radio bursts, expanding the known origins of long‑period radio transients.</Callout>
## What happened
Since 2022, astronomers have cataloged long‑period radio transients, most of which were traced to binaries of a white dwarf and a red dwarf. New research published in Nature Astronomy reports the first case where the companion resembles the Sun, marking a distinct class of sources.
In late 2024, researchers used the Murchison Widefield Array and a custom detection algorithm to scan the sky for short, periodic flashes. They recorded bursts arriving every 7.9 minutes that persisted for a six‑hour interval each day, followed by a 26‑hour silence. The regular on‑off pattern points to orbital modulation in a binary system.
The leading interpretation is that the white dwarf accretes material from the Sun‑like star’s stellar wind, triggering magnetic reconnection events that launch coherent radio pulses on the orbital timescale.
## Why it matters
The discovery widens the astrophysical contexts that can produce long‑period radio transients, showing that not only low‑mass red dwarfs but also Sun‑like stars can drive the phenomenon. This challenges existing models of magnetic interaction in compact binaries and provides a new laboratory for studying white dwarf magnetospheres. It also refines the criteria used by SETI projects to filter out natural periodic signals, reducing false‑positive rates.

## How to think about it
Treat periodic radio flashes as potential signatures of compact binaries rather than exotic phenomena. When a regular cadence is detected, cross‑match the sky position with optical and infrared catalogs to identify possible stellar companions. Incorporate orbital phase modeling to predict on‑source windows, and schedule multi‑wavelength follow‑up during predicted active periods. This framework helps distinguish natural binary interactions from other transient classes.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume all regular radio bursts are artificial; confirm with multi‑wavelength data before drawing conclusions about extraterrestrial origins.</Callout>
