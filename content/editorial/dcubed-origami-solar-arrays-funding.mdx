SpaceMinds host Mike Gruss sat down with Thomas Sinn, CEO of Danish startup Dcubed, to reveal a fresh $30 million financing round aimed at reshaping satellite power systems. The conversation framed solar panels as a kind of origami, where folding techniques could dramatically increase surface area without enlarging launch volume. Dcubed argues that this approach is essential as satellite constellations demand ever‑greater electrical power for payloads and onboard processing. If successful, the technology could set a new baseline for how space‑based energy is harvested and managed.

<Callout type="takeaway">Origami‑inspired solar arrays could become the power workhorse for the next wave of high‑capacity satellites.</Callout>

## What happened
In a recent episode of SpaceMinds recorded at Space Tech Expo Europe, Thomas Sinn announced that Dcubed closed a $30 million funding round led by European venture partners. The capital will be directed toward scaling prototype production, refining folding mechanisms, and conducting on‑orbit demonstration missions slated for 2028.

The core concept treats each solar cell as a flexible sheet that can be folded like paper, allowing a compact stowage configuration during launch and a rapid, reliable deployment once in orbit. Sinn emphasized that the design reduces mass and volume while delivering up to 30 percent more power per unit area compared with conventional rigid panels.

Industry observers noted that the funding aligns with a broader trend of modular, high‑efficiency power solutions needed for mega‑constellations and deep‑space probes that operate far from Earth’s sunlight.

## Why it matters
Satellite operators are confronting a power gap as payloads become more compute‑intensive and communication bandwidth expands. Traditional solar arrays are reaching practical limits in size and weight, prompting engineers to seek innovative packaging methods. By folding panels like origami, Dcubed promises to unlock additional wattage without proportionally increasing launch costs, potentially lowering the price per gigabit of data delivered from orbit.

The infusion of $30 million signals confidence from investors that the market will reward such breakthroughs, accelerating the timeline for commercial adoption. If the technology matures, it could reshape satellite bus architecture, influence launch vehicle design, and set new standards for energy budgeting in space missions.

## How to think about it
When evaluating whether to adopt origami‑based arrays, start by mapping your mission’s power budget against the projected increase in wattage per unit area. Compare the added mass and volume savings against the engineering effort needed to integrate a folding mechanism. Consider a phased approach: begin with a technology‑demonstration satellite to validate deployment reliability, then scale to larger constellations once performance data is verified. Keep an eye on the evolving standards for solar cell materials, as newer high‑efficiency cells can amplify the benefits of a folded architecture.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the folding system is flight‑ready without a dedicated on‑orbit test; premature integration could jeopardize mission timelines.</Callout>
