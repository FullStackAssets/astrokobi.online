A massive, 33‑solar‑mass star in a galaxy 30 million light‑years away has been flaring repeatedly in X‑rays. NASA’s Chandra X‑ray Observatory identified the star as AT 2016blu and captured a series of outbursts that began in 2026. The observations reveal that the eruptions are driven by violent mass‑loss episodes as the star approaches a critical instability threshold. Understanding this behavior helps astronomers predict the fate of the most massive stars and the types of explosions they will produce.

<Callout type="takeaway">AT 2016blu’s repeated X‑ray outbursts are powered by episodic mass‑loss as the star nears a critical instability.</Callout>

## What happened
Astronomers using Chandra focused on the spiral galaxy NGC 4559, about 30 million light‑years distant, and pinpointed a luminous, unstable star designated AT 2016blu. The star’s mass is estimated to be at least 33 times that of the Sun, placing it among the most massive known stars in the local universe.

Over several months in 2026, Chandra recorded a sequence of sharp X‑ray spikes lasting from a few days to weeks. Each spike corresponded to a sudden brightening in the optical data from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, indicating that the star expelled large amounts of material in rapid, eruptive episodes.

The combined X‑ray and optical imaging shows that the expelled gas forms expanding shells that collide with the surrounding interstellar medium, heating it to millions of degrees and producing the observed high‑energy radiation.

## Why it matters
Massive stars dominate the chemical enrichment of galaxies, and their final explosions seed the cosmos with heavy elements. By catching AT 2016blu in the act, scientists gain a rare window into the pre‑supernova instability phase that many models predict but have rarely observed. The findings also refine estimates of how often such eruptive phases occur, influencing predictions of supernova rates and the energy budget of galactic ecosystems.

## How to think about it
Treat AT 2016blu as a case study in a broader framework: (1) monitor massive stars with both high‑energy and optical surveys; (2) look for correlated X‑ray/optical spikes as signatures of violent mass loss; (3) incorporate these events into stellar evolution simulations to improve predictions of supernova progenitors. By applying this three‑step approach, researchers can systematically identify stars on the brink of collapse.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that every X‑ray flare from a massive star signals an imminent supernova; many eruptions may be part of a longer, cyclic instability cycle.</Callout>
