NASA announced Wednesday that three industry partners will receive firm‑fixed‑price contracts to design Ka‑band relay solutions for current spacecraft. The awards total $905.1 million, split among Astranis Space Technologies ($264.3 M), Global Enterprise Solutions ($340.4 M), and a third undisclosed partner. Work runs from Nov 4, 2026 through May 3, 2027 with options extending to 2029, aiming for an in‑orbit demonstration. This effort supports NASA’s plan to retire the legacy Tracking and Data Relay Satellite (TDRS) fleet while preserving service for missions that cannot retrofit their hardware.

<Callout type="takeaway">Ka‑band relay contracts aim to safeguard spacecraft communications as NASA phases out its aging relay fleet.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA selected three companies under the Next Space Technologies for Exploration Partnerships‑3 Broad Agency Announcement to develop end‑to‑end Ka‑band relay concepts. The contracts cover space, ground, launch, operations, and integration, giving NASA a pathway from concept risk reduction to a flight demonstration.

The award structure is firm‑fixed‑price with optional funding that could increase the total value if milestones are met, allowing NASA to scale investment as technology matures. The initiative is managed by the SCaN (Space Communications and Navigation) program’s Communications Services Project.

## Why it matters
Ka‑band offers higher bandwidth than the S‑band and Ku‑band currently used for relay, enabling faster data downlinks for science and exploration missions. By securing commercial partners, NASA reduces reliance on its aging TDRS constellation and spreads risk across the private sector. Successful development could set a new standard for future deep‑space and low‑Earth‑orbit missions that lack the ability to modify onboard antennas.

## How to think about it
Stakeholders should view the contracts as a phased technology incubator: first validate the concept on the ground, then demonstrate in orbit before committing to full‑scale deployment. Mission planners can begin mapping which spacecraft could benefit from Ka‑band without hardware changes, while budgeting for possible transition costs. Industry partners should align their development timelines with NASA’s option periods to maximize the chance of securing follow‑on funding.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume these services will be operational before the 2029 option period ends; development is still in concept stage.</Callout>
