NASA announced on Nov 4 that it has awarded three companies a combined $604.7 million to develop Ka‑band satellite relay concepts. The contracts, issued under the Next Space Technologies for Exploration Partnerships‑3 (NSTEP‑3) program, aim to create end‑to‑end solutions that keep existing spacecraft linked without any onboard hardware changes. By building a commercial relay capability, NASA hopes to phase out its aging Tracking and Data Relay Satellite (TDRS) fleet while preserving service continuity for missions that cannot switch to other commercial links. The effort spans space, ground, launch and operations elements, with an initial performance window through May 2027 and options extending to 2029.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s Ka‑band relay contracts could secure uninterrupted communications for legacy spacecraft while opening a market for commercial providers.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA selected three firms—Astranis Space Technologies Corp., Global Enterprise Solutions Inc., and a third undisclosed partner—to develop Ka‑band relay capability concepts compatible with current missions. Astranis received a contract valued at $264.3 million and Global Enterprise Solutions at $340.4 million; the third award’s value was not disclosed. All contracts are firm‑fixed‑price with options for additional funding up to the award limit, allowing work to progress from concept development to an in‑orbit flight demonstration.

The work is managed by NASA’s Space Communications and Navigation (SCaN) program and supports the agency’s plan to gradually retire the TDRS fleet. Ka‑band services are needed for select in‑orbit missions that cannot feasibly modify flight hardware or transition to non‑compatible commercial services, ensuring continuity without redesign.

## Why it matters
Maintaining continuous communications is essential for mission safety, data return, and operational flexibility. By fostering commercial Ka‑band relay solutions, NASA reduces reliance on its own aging infrastructure and spreads risk across industry partners. Successful demonstrations could create a new market segment, lowering costs for future missions and enabling rapid deployment of relay satellites for emerging science and exploration objectives.

## How to think about it
Treat the Ka‑band relay effort as a phased technology insertion. First, track milestone deliveries from each contractor to gauge maturity. Second, assess how the emerging services align with your mission’s communication windows and bandwidth needs. Finally, incorporate flexibility in mission design to switch between legacy TDRS and commercial Ka‑band links as the latter become operational.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the contracts will automatically fund full‑scale deployment; additional appropriations are required for later phases.</Callout>
