NASA is set to greet the Expedition 74 crew at Space Center Houston on Nov. 17, turning a routine return into a public celebration. After a 237‑day stint aboard the International Space Station, astronauts Jessica Meir, Jack Hathaway, Chris Williams, ESA’s Sophie Adenot, and Roscosmos cosmonauts Sergey Kud‑Sverchkov and Sergei Mikaev will share mission highlights. The event includes a presentation, an awards ceremony, and media interview opportunities. Bringing the crew home in front of visitors underscores the agency’s push to make spaceflight achievements visible to the public.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Expedition 74 homecoming showcases how long‑duration ISS research can be turned into a public outreach moment.</Callout>

## What happened
The public event will take place at NASA Johnson Space Center’s visitor center at 6 p.m. CST on Tuesday, Nov. 17. The six crew members—Meir, Hathaway, Williams, Adenot, Kud‑Sverchkov, and Mikaev—will present mission highlights, followed by an awards ceremony recognizing key contributors.

During their 237‑day (Meir, Hathaway, Adenot) and 241‑day (Williams, Kud‑Sverchkov, Mikaev) missions, the crew completed more than 3,800 Earth orbits, traveled over 100 million miles, and logged more than 1,400 hours of scientific research and technology demonstrations, including in‑space 3D‑printing and human‑health studies.

The three Russian and American members launched aboard Soyuz MS‑28 on Nov. 27, 2025, while the NASA‑ESA‑SpaceX crew launched on a Dragon capsule on Feb. 13 as part of Crew‑12. All returned safely, with the Soyuz trio landing in Kazakhstan on July 26 and the Dragon crew splashing down in the Pacific on Oct. 8.

## Why it matters
The event bridges the gap between complex orbital science and everyday audiences, reinforcing public support for long‑duration missions that pave the way for future lunar and Martian exploration. By showcasing research on 3D‑printed materials, blood‑flow dynamics, and pilot performance under microgravity, NASA highlights tangible benefits that can translate to medical and manufacturing advances on Earth. The visibility also boosts crew morale and inspires the next generation of engineers and scientists.
