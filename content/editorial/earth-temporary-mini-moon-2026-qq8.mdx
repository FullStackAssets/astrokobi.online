On April 2026 a small asteroid, designated 2026 QQ8, slipped into Earth’s Hill sphere and became a temporary mini‑moon. Discovered by Krisztián Sárneczky in August 2026, the rock was confirmed as natural after follow‑up observations with the UH 2.2‑m and Lowell Discovery telescopes. It will orbit Earth in an irregular path until roughly December, then resume its heliocentric journey. At about one metre across, it poses no impact threat, but its capture offers a live laboratory for studying near‑Earth objects. Understanding such transient captures helps refine models of asteroid dynamics and planetary defense.

## What happened
The Minor Planet Center reported that 2026 QQ8 was first spotted on 20 August 2026, but orbital calculations showed it entered Earth’s Hill sphere in late April. Its closest approach will be 0.0038 AU, roughly one‑and‑a‑half times the distance to the Moon, eliminating any collision concern.

Follow‑up imaging with the University of Hawaii 2.2‑meter telescope and the Lowell Discovery Telescope confirmed the object’s natural, rocky nature rather than space debris. The mini‑moon will remain bound to Earth through December 2026 before escaping back into a solar orbit.

Historical data indicate that 2026 QQ8’s orbit brings it near Earth about every ten years. In 1965 it passed close but did not enter the Hill sphere, and a similar near‑miss is predicted for 2058.

## Why it matters
Temporary captures let scientists observe how small asteroids interact with a planet’s gravity, testing predictions of orbital dynamics and the limits of the Hill sphere. The event also provides a tangible example for public outreach about near‑Earth objects and planetary defense strategies. While the rock itself is harmless, the data gathered improve risk assessments for larger, potentially hazardous asteroids.
