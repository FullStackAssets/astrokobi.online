On November 13, 2025 NASA launched the twin ESCAPADE spacecraft on a Blue Origin New Glenn rocket, aiming to study how the Sun’s wind has stripped Mars of its atmosphere. The launch was nudged later than planned by a powerful X5.1 solar flare two days earlier, underscoring the very phenomenon the probes will monitor. ESCAPADE will first park at Earth‑Sun Lagrange point 2, then use an Earth gravity assist to slingshot toward Mars, with arrival expected in September 2027. By measuring real‑time solar wind conditions at Mars, the mission promises to fill a gap in our understanding of planetary habitability. The findings will inform both future crewed missions and the search for life on exoplanets.

<Callout type="takeaway">Mars lost most of its air because the Sun’s relentless wind constantly erodes unprotected atmospheres.</Callout>

## What happened
The twin ESCAPADE probes lifted off from Cape Canaveral on a New Glenn vehicle, a launch delayed by the X5.1 flare on Nov 11, 2025. After a brief cruise to the Sun‑Earth L2 point, the spacecraft will loop back past Earth in early 2026 for a gravity‑assist maneuver that puts them on a trajectory toward Mars, targeting a September 2027 arrival. During the same period the Sun produced eight flares—seven C‑class and one B‑class—the strongest being a C4.9 from active region AR4566 on Nov 12, with AR4566 accounting for six of the eight events.

## Why it matters
The loss of atmosphere on Mars is a textbook example of how a star can render a planet barren, a process that directly impacts the viability of future human outposts and the interpretation of exoplanet spectra. Understanding the rate and variability of solar‑wind stripping helps engineers design better shielding for habitats and informs models that predict which distant worlds could retain atmospheres long enough for life. Moreover, real‑time Martian space‑weather forecasts will be essential for protecting crew and equipment during the first crewed missions to the Red Planet.

## How to think about it
Treat atmospheric erosion as a balance between a planet’s magnetic protection and the incident solar‑wind pressure; when the magnetic field is weak, as on Mars, the wind directly sputters atmospheric particles into space. For any future mission, evaluate the host star’s activity cycle, model the expected particle flux, and build redundancy into communication and power systems to survive peak events.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the ESCAPADE data will instantly solve all Martian habitability questions; the measurements are limited in time and spatial coverage, and solar activity can still interrupt data collection.</Callout>
