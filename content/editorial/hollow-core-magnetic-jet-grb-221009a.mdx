The brightest gamma‑ray burst ever recorded, GRB 221009A, lit up detectors across the globe on October 9, 2022, overwhelming many instruments. A China‑Italy collaboration has now pieced together a time‑resolved spectrum of the event using the HEPP‑H detector aboard the CSES‑01 satellite. Their analysis uncovers a distinctive ~2 MeV spectral bump and a fading narrow line near 40 MeV, suggesting a magnetically dominated jet with a hollow core. If correct, this challenges conventional fireball models and offers a new window onto the physics of extreme cosmic explosions.

<Callout type="takeaway">GRB 221009A likely produced a hollow‑core magnetic jet, revealed by unsaturated spectral features captured by a low‑efficiency detector.</Callout>

## What happened
The joint Chinese‑Italian team reconstructed the burst’s spectrum by exploiting HEPP‑H’s indirect gamma‑ray detection, which remained unsaturated during the peak. By applying a dynamic Monte Carlo correction and cross‑calibrating with GECAM‑C and Fermi data, they recovered the intrinsic emission across six time intervals. Across all intervals a ~2 MeV bump appears, requiring an extra component beyond the standard Band function and accounting for roughly 6 % of the burst’s gamma‑ray energy. Additionally, a narrow line that starts near 40 MeV and drifts downward was identified, possibly arising from electron‑positron annihilation.

## Why it matters
If the spectral bumps indeed trace a hollow‑core, magnetically dominated jet, they provide direct evidence that magnetic fields can shape GRB outflows more than previously thought. This influences models of jet acceleration, particle energization, and the role of thermal fireballs versus Poynting‑flux jets. The findings also demonstrate the value of low‑efficiency, unsaturated detectors for capturing the brightest phases of transient events, informing the design of future high‑energy observatories.

## How to think about it
When evaluating extreme transients, prioritize instruments that can survive saturation and complement them with high‑efficiency detectors. Apply rigorous correction pipelines—Monte Carlo simulations and cross‑calibration—to retrieve the true spectrum. Treat unexpected spectral components as clues to underlying jet composition, but test them against multiple physical models before drawing firm conclusions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the hollow‑core jet interpretation is definitive; alternative explanations for the spectral features remain viable.</Callout>
