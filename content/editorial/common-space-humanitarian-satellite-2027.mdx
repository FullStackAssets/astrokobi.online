Common Space, a nonprofit focused on open geospatial data, announced today that its first humanitarian satellite will lift off in July 2027. The 32‑inch, 85‑lb NewSat Mark V, built by Argentine‑U.S. firm Satellogic, will deliver high‑resolution Earth imagery to first responders, journalists, scientists and human‑rights investigators. By operating on a ‘club‑good’ model, the organization aims to share infrastructure and costs across a broad community, keeping data affordable and accessible. The launch will ride on a SpaceX rideshare mission, marking the first dedicated humanitarian satellite in orbit.

<Callout type="takeaway">Common Space’s first humanitarian satellite will bring affordable, high‑resolution Earth imagery to responders and watchdogs worldwide.</Callout>

## What happened
On Nov. 19, Common Space confirmed that Satellogic will build and operate the inaugural spacecraft of its planned six‑satellite constellation. The selected platform, NewSat Mark V, measures 32 inches (82 cm) in length and weighs 85 lb (38.5 kg). From low‑Earth orbit it can resolve ground features as small as 27.5 inches (70 cm), and Satellogic’s proprietary algorithms boost that to a super‑resolution of 19.7 inches (50 cm).

The satellite is slated for a July 2027 launch on a SpaceX rideshare flight, with Satellogic handling both launch preparations and at‑least‑five‑year operations. Common Space intends to expand the constellation to six microsatellites, each delivering low‑cost, high‑quality imagery for humanitarian and public‑interest purposes.

## Why it matters
Providing high‑resolution, affordable imagery breaks the monopoly of proprietary Earth‑observation data that often costs millions and remains inaccessible to NGOs and journalists. Timely, detailed views of disaster zones, conflict areas, and environmental crises can improve response coordination, evidence gathering, and policy advocacy. By institutionalizing a “club‑good” framework, Common Space could set a precedent for open‑access space infrastructure that balances sustainability with public benefit.

## How to think about it
Humanitarian organizations should map their data needs against the satellite’s resolution and revisit procurement plans to incorporate this low‑cost source. Analysts must validate the “super‑resolution” outputs against ground truth before making critical decisions. Policymakers can use the club‑good model as a template for future public‑interest space projects, ensuring governance structures protect against misuse while encouraging broader participation.
