Black holes are famously indestructible, but a new theoretical paper suggests that a black hole spinning close to its maximal rate could, in principle, split into two smaller holes. The authors – Francesco Sannino, Giacomo Cacciapaglia, Manuel Del Piano and Vania Vellucci – used quantum‑field calculations to map a “pair‑of‑pants” horizon topology that would allow such a division. They find that the two fragments must remain separated, a condition that only extreme rotation seems to satisfy. If this fragmentation were possible, it would rewrite our understanding of horizon dynamics and entropy. The idea remains speculative, yet it opens a concrete pathway to test the limits of general relativity.

<Callout type="takeaway">A near‑maximally rotating black hole could, under very specific conditions, split into two separate horizons.</Callout>

## What happened
The European team performed analytical calculations within quantum field theory, focusing on how a rotating Kerr black hole might evolve if its angular momentum approached the theoretical limit. Their models show that the event horizon could deform into a trouser‑shaped, or “pair‑of‑pants,” configuration that eventually pinches off into two distinct horizons.

A crucial part of the analysis is the requirement that the two newborn black holes stay apart rather than immediately re‑merge. This separation only persists when the spin parameter is extremely close to one, otherwise gravitational attraction forces the pieces back together. Additionally, the authors highlight entropy as a major obstacle: splitting a non‑rotating black hole would reduce its total entropy, violating the second law of thermodynamics, so only a highly spinning hole could potentially overcome this barrier.

## Why it matters
If black holes can fragment, the process would generate a novel class of gravitational‑wave signatures distinct from mergers, offering a new observational window into extreme gravity. It also forces theorists to reconcile horizon topology changes with the laws of black‑hole thermodynamics, potentially shedding light on quantum gravity effects near the Planck scale. Finally, the scenario provides a concrete test case for alternative theories that predict deviations from classical general relativity in the high‑spin regime.

## How to think about it
When evaluating exotic black‑hole scenarios, start by checking the spin parameter: only near‑extremal values open the fragmentation window. Next, assess entropy: any proposed split must not lower the total horizon entropy unless a compensating mechanism is identified. Finally, consider observational consequences—look for short‑duration, high‑frequency gravitational‑wave bursts that differ from standard merger waveforms.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that black‑hole splitting is a common astrophysical event; the required conditions are extreme and have not been observed.</Callout>
