Record numbers of rockets reached orbit in 2026, with the FAA licensing 187 launches. Yet satellite operators are still scrambling for suitable rides. The shortage isn’t just about quantity; it’s about getting the right vehicle to the right orbit at the right time. As the space economy pushes past $600 billion, this mismatch threatens growth and resilience.

<Callout type="takeaway">Launch diversity, not just more rockets, is the bottleneck for a flexible space economy.</Callout>

## What happened
The launch cadence hit a historic high, but most of the activity clustered around a handful of frequently used rockets that target similar low‑Earth‑orbit slots. Exolaunch CEO Robert Sproles described the demand curve as far steeper than the supply curve, highlighting months‑long waits for many operators. Meanwhile, the FAA’s data show 187 licensed launches in fiscal year 2026, up from fewer than 100 a decade earlier.

Market research from Novaspace placed the global space economy at $626 billion in 2025 and projected a 5.5 % annual growth rate through 2034, aiming for a valuation above $1 trillion. The same analysis points to consolidation and tighter security requirements as drivers for more integrated launch services. Policy goals, such as the U.S. administration’s target of 1,000 launches and re‑entries per year by 2030, further pressure the industry to expand capacity.

## Why it matters
When a launch cannot match a satellite’s orbital destination, operators face delayed deployments, higher operational costs, and reduced revenue streams. Nations that rely on timely satellite constellations for communications, navigation, or defense may see strategic gaps. A more varied launch ecosystem also spreads risk, making the overall space infrastructure more resilient to single‑point failures.

## How to think about it
Start by mapping the payload’s orbital parameters, launch mass, and schedule flexibility. Then inventory the current launch portfolio—both legacy heavy‑lift rockets and emerging small‑sat launchers—to find the closest fit. Diversify contracts across multiple providers to avoid reliance on a single launch cadence. Finally, incorporate schedule buffers and contingency plans, treating launch choice as a risk‑management decision rather than a fixed cost.

<Callout type="warning">Do not commit critical, high‑value payloads to unproven launch services without thorough reliability data and insurance coverage.</Callout>
