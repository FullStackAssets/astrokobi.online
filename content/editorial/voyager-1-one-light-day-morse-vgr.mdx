On November 17, amateur astronomers across the globe turned their flashlights into a giant Morse‑code transmitter, beaming the letters V‑G‑R into the night sky. The pattern was captured in a long‑exposure image by the Emirates Astronomical Observatory and decoded as the call sign of Voyager 1, humanity’s most distant probe. Two days later, at 10:16:07 UTC on November 18, Voyager 1 crossed the one‑light‑day threshold, a distance of roughly 26 billion kilometres from Earth. The event links a simple public outreach activity to a measurable milestone in interstellar travel, reminding us how vast and slow‑moving our cosmic backyard truly is.

<Callout type="takeaway">Voyager 1’s one‑light‑day milestone was celebrated by a global Morse‑code flash, linking public imagination to a concrete interstellar distance.</Callout>

## What happened
On the night of November 17, organizers invited participants to flash a handheld light on and off, creating dashes and dots that formed the Morse sequence V‑G‑R. The Emirates Astronomical Observatory recorded the sequence in a time‑exposure photograph, later confirming the pattern matched Voyager 1’s designated call sign.

Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is now about 26 billion kilometres from Earth, or one light‑day away. At 10:16:07 UTC on November 18, mission controllers announced that the probe had officially reached this one‑light‑day distance, marking the first time a human‑made object has been a full day’s light‑travel time from our planet.

At the current rate of one light‑day per 50 years, Voyager 1 would need roughly 18 250 years to travel a full light‑year, underscoring the immense timescales of interstellar exploration.

## Why it matters
The milestone turns an abstract astronomical unit into a concrete, human‑scale experience, showing the public that interstellar distances, while enormous, can be celebrated through simple, participatory actions. It also highlights the extraordinary longevity of Voyager 1, prompting discussions about how future probes might be designed for even longer missions and how we communicate with them across vast expanses.

## How to think about it
Use distance milestones like the one‑light‑day mark as teaching tools: compare them to familiar scales (e.g., Earth‑Moon distance), discuss the speed of light, and explore the engineering challenges of maintaining a spacecraft for decades. When planning future missions, consider how public engagement can be woven into scientific objectives without compromising data collection.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the Morse‑code flash was received by Voyager 1; the spacecraft cannot detect visible light and the event was purely symbolic.</Callout>
