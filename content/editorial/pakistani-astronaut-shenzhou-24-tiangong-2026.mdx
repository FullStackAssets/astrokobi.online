China is set to launch its 18th crewed mission, Shenzhou‑24, on November 25, 2026, and for the first time a foreign astronaut will ride alongside two Chinese taikonauts to the Tiangong space station. The selected Pakistani astronaut—chosen from a pair of SUPARCO candidates—will spend about a week aboard the Tianhe core module before returning on Shenzhou‑23. This marks the inaugural joint flight between China’s space program and another nation’s astronaut corps. The milestone signals a shift toward broader international participation in China’s low‑Earth‑orbit activities.

<Callout type="takeaway">A Pakistani astronaut will become the first non‑Chinese crew member on Tiangong, highlighting China’s move toward international partnership.</Callout>

## What happened
Shenzhou‑24 will lift off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center on a Long March 2F rocket, carrying a commander, a pilot, and a payload specialist from Pakistan. The mission will dock with the Tianhe core module, joining the station’s existing crew for a week‑long stay.

The partnership traces back to a bilateral agreement signed in February 2025, under which SUPARCO astronauts received training in China. After a multi‑stage selection that concluded in April 2026, Muhammad Zeeshan Ali and Khurram Daud were shortlisted, with one slated for flight and the other as backup.

Following the week aboard Tiangong, the Pakistani astronaut will return to Earth aboard the Shenzhou‑23 spacecraft, which is scheduled to depart the station shortly after the visit. The flight also arrives as India’s Gaganyaan crewed launch is projected for the second quarter of 2027, giving Pakistan a brief lead in South Asian human spaceflight.

## Why it matters
The mission signals China’s willingness to open its low‑Earth‑orbit platform to non‑Chinese partners, a step toward the kind of multinational cooperation seen on the ISS. For Pakistan, the flight provides a rapid path to human‑spaceflight capability and a prestige boost that narrows the gap with India’s emerging program. Regionally, the event may intensify competition while also creating a diplomatic bridge that could pave the way for future joint scientific experiments or technology sharing.

## How to think about it
Treat the flight as a diplomatic signal rather than a permanent policy shift. Track subsequent crew‑exchange agreements to gauge whether China is building a broader partner network. For policymakers, weigh the prestige benefits against the strategic implications of deepening ties with a rising space power. For enthusiasts, consider the scientific opportunities that could arise from joint experiments during the week‑long stay.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume this single flight guarantees a steady stream of international partners for China’s station.</Callout>
