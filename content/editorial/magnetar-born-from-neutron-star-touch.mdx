Magnetars—neutron stars with magnetic fields a thousand times stronger than typical pulsars—have long puzzled astronomers. A new simulation study shows that when two low‑mass neutron stars merge without exceeding the Tolman‑Oppenheimer‑Volkoff limit, the contact region becomes violently turbulent and can boost the magnetic field by more than three hundredfold. This pathway creates a fresh magnetar in milliseconds, linking binary mergers directly to some of the most energetic transient phenomena. Understanding this mechanism reshapes how we interpret fast radio bursts and soft gamma‑ray repeaters that follow gravitational‑wave events.

<Callout type="takeaway">A low‑mass neutron‑star merger can instantly forge a magnetar through Kelvin‑Helmholtz‑driven magnetic amplification.</Callout>

## What happened
The study began by noting that typical neutron stars possess magnetic fields about 100,000 times stronger than the strongest fields we can generate on Earth, and roughly 1 % of them—magnetars—exhibit fields a thousand times greater still. Most binary neutron‑star collisions produce a black hole because the combined mass exceeds the Tolman‑Oppenheimer‑Volkoff (TOV) limit of roughly 2.6 solar masses. However, if both stars are low‑mass enough that their total stays below this limit, the merger can leave behind a single, massive neutron star.

Using the Japanese supercomputer FUGAKU, researchers simulated equal‑mass binaries with initial magnetic fields around 3 trillion gauss. Within milliseconds of contact, the shear layer between the stars triggered a Kelvin–Helmholtz instability, turning the flow turbulent. This turbulence mixed the neutron fluid rapidly, dragging and stretching magnetic field lines, which amplified the field by more than 300 times compared with the pre‑merger value.

## Why it matters
If mergers can generate magnetars on such short timescales, they provide a natural engine for the powerful soft gamma‑ray repeaters and some fast radio bursts that follow gravitational‑wave detections. The mechanism also offers a missing link in population models that struggle to explain why only a small fraction of neutron stars become magnetars. Moreover, the amplified magnetic field could influence the post‑merger spin‑down and electromagnetic afterglow, giving observers a new diagnostic for identifying merger‑born magnetars.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a neutron‑star merger, first check the combined mass against the TOV limit. If the system is sub‑threshold, model the shear layer for Kelvin‑Helmholtz growth and include a turbulent dynamo term in magnetohydrodynamic simulations. Observationally, look for a delayed, magnetar‑like X‑ray or radio flare following a gravitational‑wave signal, which could signal a merger‑born magnetar.

<Callout type="warning">Do not extrapolate this amplification mechanism to typical high‑mass mergers that promptly collapse into black holes.</Callout>
